New Treatment Pathways Emerge for Acute Myeloid Leukemia

"Scientists are developing new drugs and tests for acute myeloid leukemia (AML), an aggressive blood cancer. One oral drug has entered early human testing, while laboratory discoveries may help match treatments to patients. Better testing could also expand access to specialized care at community hospitals."

A new oral drug called AMX-883 began Phase 1 testing in adults with relapsed or refractory AML and high-risk myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) [a bone-marrow disorder that can progress to AML], after FDA clearance of its investigational new drug application in June 2026.
AMX-883 uses targeted protein degradation [a technique that marks a specific protein for the cell to break down] to remove BRD9, a protein that helps leukemia cells survive and resist treatment.
Researchers reported that a common AML gene mutation called NPM1 disrupts the correct placement of proteins during cell division, in laboratory studies.
A new sample-handling approach stabilizes bone marrow samples for longer, which could let community hospitals ship them for specialized testing.
AML is aggressive, with a five-year survival of roughly one-third of patients, and many develop resistance to current treatments (general medical guidance).
In preclinical studies, AMX-883 showed activity across different AML subtypes regardless of specific gene mutations, which the developer hopes could help more patients — though this has not yet been shown in people.
Understanding how NPM1 misplaces proteins could, in time, suggest new ways to make existing therapies work better.
Wider access to measurable residual disease (MRD) [a test that detects tiny amounts of leukemia remaining after treatment] testing would let more patients be monitored closely after treatment.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

AMX-883 (Amphista Therapeutics): an oral BRD9 degrader in the Phase 1 BRAMLIE trial for relapsed/refractory AML and high-risk MDS; expected to enroll about 54 patients.

Venetoclax is a standard targeted AML treatment (often combined with other agents), though resistance can emerge in some patients (general medical guidance).

Menin inhibitors are being studied for KMT2A-rearranged AML and other fusion-driven leukemias (independent fact-check: the draft's claim that 'imatinib' is used for AML subtypes was removed — imatinib is used for CML and Philadelphia-positive ALL, not standard AML) (general medical guidance).

Preclinically, AMX-883 showed synergy with venetoclax and prevented the emergence of venetoclax resistance in laboratory models.

Evidence Uncertainty

The BRAMLIE trial plans to enroll about 54 patients across multiple sites to assess safety, pharmacokinetics, and preliminary effectiveness; no clinical results have been reported yet.

The NPM1 cell-division findings come from laboratory cells and patient samples and are not yet a proven treatment approach.

The bone-marrow stabilization approach is reported to extend sample processing from about 72 hours to as long as 90 days in early testing.

Long-term survival data and any broader regulatory approval for AMX-883 are not yet available.

What To Watch

Early safety results from the BRAMLIE Phase 1 trial.

FDA decisions on new AML therapies and expanded indications for existing drugs (general medical guidance).

Wider rollout of stabilized bone-marrow testing to community and rural hospitals.

Further research on NPM1-related protein positioning and other new approaches (general medical guidance).
