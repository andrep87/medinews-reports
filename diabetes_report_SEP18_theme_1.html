First New Drug in 30 Years for Kidney Disease in Type 1 Diabetes: Finerenone (Kerendia) Wins FDA Approval

"The FDA has approved finerenone (Kerendia) for adults with chronic kidney disease (CKD) associated with type 1 diabetes, marking the first new treatment for this condition in over three decades. The once-daily pill reduces albumin in the urine (UACR), a key marker of kidney damage, and is expected to slow progression to kidney failure, offering a long-awaited option for the 20–30% of people with type 1 diabetes who develop CKD."

The FDA granted priority review approval to finerenone (Kerendia) for adults with chronic kidney disease (CKD) [reduced kidney function] and type 1 diabetes, based on the phase III FINE-ONE trial.
FINE-ONE enrolled 242 adults with type 1 diabetes, CKD, and albuminuria [excess protein in the urine, a sign of kidney damage] who were already on standard ACE inhibitor or ARB therapy [blood pressure drugs that protect the kidneys].
Finerenone reduced UACR [urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, a measure of kidney damage] by 22% at 3 months and 28% at 6 months versus placebo (p=0.0001). 68% of treated patients achieved at least a 30% UACR reduction vs. 47% on placebo.
This is finerenone's third FDA indication: previously approved for CKD in type 2 diabetes (2021) and heart failure with LVEF ≥40% (2025).
Roughly 20–30% of U.S. adults with type 1 diabetes develop CKD, which dramatically raises risks of kidney failure, heart complications, and death.
Until now, no new kidney-protective drug had been approved for type 1 diabetes in over 30 years; care relied on blood pressure and glucose control alone.
Finerenone blocks overactive mineralocorticoid receptors that drive inflammation, fibrosis, and kidney damage — a different pathway than ACE/ARB drugs.
The approval uses UACR reduction as a surrogate endpoint, supported by trials in type 2 diabetes where UACR drops predicted slower CKD progression.
Adults with type 1 diabetes and CKD (eGFR 25 to <90 mL/min/1.73m²) with albuminuria (UACR 200–5000).
Patients already on maximally tolerated ACE inhibitor or ARB therapy (standard background treatment).
Not for patients with eGFR [a measure of kidney filtering capacity] below 25, UACR above 5,000, adrenal insufficiency, or those on strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (such as ketoconazole or clarithromycin). Serum potassium should be monitored as hyperkalemia [high potassium] is a known risk (general medical guidance).
Pregnancy and pediatric use not yet studied; ongoing pediatric trials are planned.
Ask your endocrinologist or nephrologist: "Do I have albuminuria (UACR tested in past year)? If so, am I a candidate for finerenone?"
Request UACR and eGFR testing at your next visit if not done recently — these determine eligibility and baseline for monitoring.
If starting finerenone, your doctor will schedule potassium and eGFR checks — the prescribing label recommends monitoring, particularly in the first weeks (general medical guidance).
Discuss sick-day rules with your care team: holding finerenone during acute illness, dehydration, or inability to eat/drink normally may reduce hyperkalemia risk (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • Hope: First disease-modifying kidney drug for T1D in 30 years; may delay dialysis/transplant for a significant high-risk population.
  • Reality: Benefit proven on surrogate marker (UACR) not hard outcomes; requires potassium monitoring; not a cure — slows progression but doesn't reverse established CKD.

The Reality

  • Access: Pharmacy benefit expected; prior authorization likely; cost-sharing varies — ask about manufacturer copay programs if uninsured or underinsured (general medical guidance).
  • Not for everyone: Excludes advanced CKD (eGFR <25), high potassium, certain drug interactions; shared decision-making essential.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Finerenone (Kerendia, Bayer): non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist; 10 mg or 20 mg once daily based on eGFR; approved for T1D CKD (2026), T2D CKD (2021), HF LVEF ≥40% (2025).

Standard background therapy: ACE inhibitors or ARBs remain first-line kidney protection; finerenone adds to, not replaces, these (general medical guidance).

SGLT2 inhibitors [a class of drugs that lower blood sugar and protect kidneys]: approved for CKD in type 2 diabetes but not type 1 due to ketoacidosis risk; studies in type 1 diabetes with careful monitoring are ongoing (general medical guidance).

Pipeline: FINEOVATE program includes 12 phase III studies across CKD and heart failure, including pediatric CKD trials.

Evidence Uncertainty

FINE-ONE: 242 patients, 6-month double-blind RCT; primary endpoint UACR reduction met (28% at 6 months, p=0.0001); safety consistent with T2D trials.

Hyperkalemia occurred in 10.1% on finerenone vs 3.3% placebo; 1.7% discontinued for hyperkalemia; no new safety signals in T1D.

Uncertainty: The trial was not large enough to detect hard outcomes (dialysis, kidney function decline, cardiovascular events, death); long-term kidney benefit is extrapolated from type 2 diabetes trials (FIDELIO-DKD, FIGARO-DKD). Pooled analyses of those trials showed UACR reductions explained more than 80% of finerenone's observed kidney benefit.

eGFR dipped initially (-5.6 vs -2.7 mL/min/1.73m² at 6 months) but reversed after washout, suggesting hemodynamic effect not permanent damage.

What To Watch

Long-term FINE-ONE extensions and real-world evidence on dialysis-free survival, CV events, and mortality in T1D.

Pediatric CKD trial results (part of FINEOVATE program) — potential expansion to younger patients.

Comparative effectiveness vs SGLT2 inhibitors in T1D (if SGLT2s gain T1D CKD indication with mitigation strategies).

Guidelines updates: ADA/KDIGO/ESRD network recommendations on positioning finerenone in T1D CKD treatment algorithms.
