Discovery of 18 Novel Bacterial Species Links Oral Microbiome to Aggressive Prostate Cancer

"Researchers analyzing discarded DNA sequencing data from 194 prostate cancer patients (2,176 sequencing datasets) identified 18 previously unknown bacterial species, most originating from the mouth. This provides the first genomic tools to study whether specific oral bacteria drive aggressive prostate cancer, opening a potential new avenue for detection and prevention."

The Pan Prostate Cancer Group (PPCG), a global collaboration of researchers from nine countries, analyzed 2,176 whole-genome sequencing datasets from saliva, tumor, and blood samples of 194 prostate cancer patients.
Instead of discarding DNA sequences that did not match the human genome (normally filtered out as "noise"), the team used metagenomic assembly to reconstruct 249 near-complete bacterial genomes.
Twenty-four bacterial genomes did not match any known species; 18 were confirmed as entirely new species and given provisional names (e.g., Candidatus Porphyromonas leptis, Candidatus Treponema oralis, Candidatus Nanosyncoccus prostatocharitus).
Most newly identified bacteria are oral species, particularly Porphyromonas (the genus containing the main gum disease bacterium) and Treponema, supporting a potential "oral-prostate axis."
Previous research showed men carrying certain anaerobic oral bacteria were more than twice as likely to see their prostate cancer progress, but the specific species involved were unknown because they could not be grown in the lab or detected individually.
By reconstructing full genomes, researchers can now develop species-specific DNA tests, predict how to culture each bacterium, and study their genes to determine if any directly contribute to cancer aggression.
This transforms the field from observing vague bacterial "groups" to investigating specific species—asking whether one drives aggressive disease and whether targeting it changes outcomes.
Unlike tumor genetics (which we cannot change), bacteria are potentially modifiable: if a species proves causal, antibiotics, vaccines, or oral hygiene interventions could prevent or treat aggressive prostate cancer.
All men with prostate cancer, particularly those with aggressive or high-risk disease, may eventually benefit from microbiome-based risk stratification.
Men with gum disease (periodontitis) or poor oral health are of special interest, as Porphyromonas gingivalis (the main gum disease bacterium) has been found in both dental plaque and prostate fluid from the same patients.
Researchers and clinicians now have the genomic tools to study the oral-prostate connection in specific patient cohorts, including those on active surveillance or with biochemical recurrence.
Maintaining good oral hygiene (brushing, flossing, regular dental cleanings) is sound general health advice; it is not proven to prevent prostate cancer, though it reduces gum inflammation and oral bacterial load (general medical guidance).
You may wish to ask your urologist or oncologist whether any clinical trials are recruiting for microbiome sampling (saliva, urine, tissue) alongside standard genomic testing (general medical guidance).
If you have advanced prostate cancer and are undergoing liquid biopsy or tissue sequencing, you may wish to ask whether the lab analyzes non-human sequences for microbial signatures, keeping in mind this is mostly research-stage (general medical guidance).
It helps to understand this is early discovery science: no clinical tests or treatments based on these bacteria exist yet.

The Hope

  • Hope: If a specific bacterium drives aggression, a simple antibiotic or oral health intervention could prevent lethal prostate cancer — a rare modifiable factor in this disease.
  • Reality: Most microbiome-cancer links turn out to be associative, not causal; years of mechanistic work are needed before any clinical application.

The Reality

  • Hope: Species-specific tests now enable large retrospective studies linking bacterial presence to outcomes in existing biobanks.
  • Reality: Commercial tests are years away; insurance will not cover microbiome profiling for prostate cancer risk until clinical utility is proven in prospective trials.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

No treatments targeting these bacteria exist yet; the discovery is a foundational step enabling future therapeutic development.

The UEA team developed a validated DNA test to detect one of the new species (Candidatus Nanosyncoccus prostatocharitus) and predicted culture conditions for all 18 species.

The PPCG plans a series of 10 publications from their 2,000+ tumor dataset, with this bacterial study as the first — subsequent papers will explore tumor genetics, epigenetics, and clinical correlations.

Parallel research in colorectal cancer (Fusobacterium nucleatum) shows oral bacteria can travel with tumors to distant sites and that antibiotics can reduce both bacteria and tumor growth in models — a potential roadmap for prostate cancer.

Evidence Uncertainty

Strength: Large international consortium (2,000+ tumors, 9 countries); rigorous metagenomic assembly from existing data at zero extra cost; 18 novel species genetically validated (one matches a known species almost exactly, confirming method accuracy).

Limitation: Bacterial DNA found in sequencing data does not prove live bacteria are present in the prostate tumor or that they cause cancer — they could be contaminants from blood/saliva or passenger organisms.

Limitation: Most new species are anaerobic oral bacteria; their abundance may simply reflect high oral bacterial load rather than prostate tropism.

Causality completely unknown: "We are not claiming these bacteria cause prostate cancer" (Prof. Colin Cooper, PPCG co-leader); functional studies in models and isolation from patient tissues are needed next.

What To Watch

Isolation and culture of the 18 new species from patient samples to confirm they are viable in the prostate environment.

Prospective studies testing whether detection of specific species (e.g., Candidatus Porphyromonas leptis) in saliva, urine, or blood predicts progression in men on active surveillance or after treatment.

Interventional trials of oral hygiene improvement, targeted antibiotics, or vaccines in high-risk men with specific bacterial signatures.

Integration of microbiome data with tumor genomics in the PPCG's upcoming 9 publications to define molecular subtypes of prostate cancer.

Regulatory guidance on whether microbiome biomarkers can be developed as companion diagnostics for prostate cancer therapies.
