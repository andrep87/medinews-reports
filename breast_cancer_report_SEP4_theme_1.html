New First-Line Standard for HER2-Positive Metastatic Breast Cancer Extends Disease Control Beyond Three Years

"The European Commission and FDA have approved trastuzumab deruxtecan (Enhertu) plus pertuzumab as the first new first-line treatment for HER2-positive (cancer with high amounts of a growth-promoting protein) metastatic (cancer that has spread) breast cancer in over a decade. This antibody-drug conjugate combination extends median progression-free survival (how long until cancer worsens) by about 14 months (from 26.9 to 40.7 months), shifting a highly effective later-line therapy to the very start of metastatic treatment."

The DESTINY-Breast09 trial tested Enhertu plus pertuzumab against the standard treatment (taxane, trastuzumab, and pertuzumab, called THP) in 1,157 people with HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer who had not been treated before.
The Enhertu-pertuzumab combination lowered the risk of the cancer growing or causing death by 44% (hazard ratio 0.56). Median time without disease progression was 40.7 months with Enhertu-pertuzumab vs 26.9 months with THP.
The U.S. FDA approved this regimen in December 2025, and the European Commission approved it in September 2026 for first-line use, no matter the hormone receptor status.
ESMO guidelines now list this combination as a Category IA first-line option, their highest recommendation level.
For over 10 years, the standard treatment (THP) controlled cancer for a median of about 2 years. This new approval extends that to over 3 years, so more patients stay progression-free longer.
Moving an antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) to first-line treatment is a major change. Instead of saving the strongest HER2-targeted drug for after other treatments fail, doctors can now use it early when patients are healthier and more likely to benefit.
Enhertu delivers chemotherapy inside cancer cells, while pertuzumab blocks HER2 on the cell surface. Together they attack the cancer in two different ways at the same time.
Longer initial disease control may help preserve quality of life, delay the need for more treatments, and allow more patients to be well enough for later treatment options.
Adults with HER2-positive (IHC 3+ or ISH+) breast cancer that cannot be removed by surgery or has spread, and who have not had chemotherapy or HER2-targeted therapy for metastatic disease.
Patients who had HER2-targeted therapy before surgery or after surgery can still get this treatment if it has been more than 6 months since they finished that therapy.
This approval covers both hormone receptor-positive and hormone receptor-negative HER2-positive breast cancer, so it applies to a wide range of HER2-positive patients.
This does not apply to HER2-low or HER2-ultralow disease, which have their own separate Enhertu approvals.
If you are newly diagnosed with HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer, ask your oncologist if Enhertu plus pertuzumab is the recommended first-line treatment for you.
Make sure your tumor's HER2 status has been tested with current standards (IHC 3+ or ISH+), because this decides if you are eligible.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Trastuzumab deruxtecan (Enhertu, from Daiichi Sankyo/AstraZeneca) is a HER2-directed antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) with a topoisomerase I inhibitor payload. It is approved for several HER2-positive and HER2-low breast cancer settings.

Pertuzumab (Perjeta, Genentech/Roche) is used in combination with trastuzumab-based regimens.

The DESTINY-Breast09 trial also has a blinded Enhertu-only arm that is still ongoing. Its results will show whether pertuzumab adds extra benefit.

T-DXd is also approved after surgery for high-risk early HER2-positive breast cancer that still has residual disease after neoadjuvant therapy, based on the DESTINY-Breast05 trial.

Evidence Uncertainty

The DESTINY-Breast09 trial randomized 1,157 patients 1:1:1. The main endpoint was progression-free survival reviewed by independent experts. The hazard ratio was 0.56 (95% CI 0.44–0.71), P < .00001.

The safety profile was consistent with known side effects of each drug, and no new safety signals were identified.

Overall survival data are not yet mature. Longer follow-up is needed to see if the progression-free survival benefit leads to longer life.

It is still unknown how much pertuzumab adds to Enhertu alone, because the Enhertu-only arm of the trial is still blinded.
