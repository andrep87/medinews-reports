ctDNA Blood Tests Are Changing How Doctors Watch for Colon Cancer Return

"New blood tests that detect tiny bits of cancer DNA (called ctDNA) may help doctors decide who needs chemotherapy after surgery and who can safely avoid it. Early studies show promise, but results are mixed, and larger trials are needed before these tests become routine. Patients should ask whether ctDNA testing is appropriate for their situation."

Researchers tested blood samples from patients with early-stage rectal and colon cancer to look for ctDNA [circulating tumor DNA — tiny fragments of cancer DNA shed into the blood] after treatment.
In the rectal cancer trial CO.28 (also called NEO), ctDNA was detectable in 45.8% of patients before chemotherapy and fell to 8.7% afterward.
In stage II colon cancer studies, patients who still had ctDNA after surgery had substantially worse recurrence-free survival than those who cleared it.
Trials testing whether ctDNA can safely guide (reduce or intensify) chemotherapy have had mixed results, and one large ctDNA-guided trial was formally negative after early closure left it underpowered (independent fact-check: the draft attributed a specific negative result to PEGASUS; the formally-negative ctDNA-guided trial in the sources is CIRCULATE-US/NRG-GI008).
ctDNA tests could help spare some patients from chemotherapy they do not need, and its side effects.
They could also identify high-risk patients who may need more intensive treatment.
The tests use a simple blood draw and can add information alongside biopsies and scans.
However, not all studies show a clear benefit from acting on ctDNA, so doctors are still learning how best to use it.
Patients with early-stage (stage II or III) colon or rectal cancer after surgery.
Patients being considered for adjuvant chemotherapy after tumor removal.
Patients with node-negative rectal cancer who may avoid major surgery if chemotherapy works well.
People whose tumors can be tracked using tumor-informed or tissue-free ctDNA assays.

The Hope

  • ctDNA testing is promising but not yet standard of care for all colorectal cancer patients (general medical guidance).
  • Tests may miss some cancers or cause anxiety if results are unclear (general medical guidance).

The Reality

  • Acting on ctDNA to change treatment is still being validated; its prognostic value is clearer than its power to guide treatment changes.
  • Benefits shown so far are often measured in recurrence-free time, not guaranteed cures (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

ctDNA tests such as Guardant Reveal and Natera Signatera detect cancer DNA in the blood to flag minimal residual disease after surgery (Signatera is tumor-informed, designed from each patient's tumor) (general medical guidance).

CO.28 (NEO) tested ctDNA in node-negative rectal cancer patients receiving chemotherapy before local excision.

CIRCULATE and CIRCULATE-Japan studied ctDNA to guide chemotherapy decisions in stage II colon cancer.

The PEGASUS trial used liquid biopsy to guide post-surgical treatment in MSS stage III and high-risk stage II colon cancer (general medical guidance).

Evidence Uncertainty

In the CO.28 rectal cancer study, ctDNA was detected before chemotherapy in 45.8% of patients and in 8.7% afterward (58 enrolled; 53 analyzed).

Across stage II colon cancer data, ctDNA-positive patients after surgery had markedly lower disease-free survival than ctDNA-negative patients, confirming ctDNA as a strong prognostic marker.

The DYNAMIC trial showed ctDNA-guided care cut chemotherapy use (from about 28% to 15%) with similar recurrence-free survival; CIRCULATE-US was formally negative after premature closure, so ctDNA-guided de-escalation is not yet proven.

Small study sizes, varying blood-draw timing, and missing follow-up samples limit current conclusions (general medical guidance).

What To Watch

Watch for results from larger phase III trials of ctDNA-guided treatment.

Look for FDA approval of more ctDNA assays for colorectal cancer monitoring.

Watch for guideline updates from ASCO or NCCN on ctDNA use.

Monitor ongoing studies combining ctDNA results with standard pathology factors.
