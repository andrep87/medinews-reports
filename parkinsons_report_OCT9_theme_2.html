Earlier DBS Surgery May Preserve Thinking in Parkinson's Patients With a GBA1 Mutation

"A multinational study of 343 patients found that for people carrying a GBA1 mutation (a common genetic risk factor in Parkinson's), deep brain stimulation (DBS) done within about 7-8 years of diagnosis was linked to preserved thinking skills, while later surgery was linked to faster mental decline. Timing did not affect thinking in non-carriers, which is why the authors stress early genetic testing to guide surgical planning. This is an observational study, so it shows an association, not proof of cause."

Researchers analyzed 343 Parkinson's patients across 10 US and European centers who had bilateral subthalamic nucleus DBS [deep brain stimulation, a surgery that places thin electrodes in a movement-control area on both sides of the brain].
Among the 91 patients carrying a GBA1 mutation, those who had DBS within about 7-8 years of diagnosis held their thinking scores steady, while those operated on later declined significantly faster.
Among the 252 non-carriers, the timing of surgery made no difference to thinking scores.
The 7-8 year pattern held regardless of how severe the mutation was; timing, not mutation severity, was the decisive factor.
It helps resolve a long-standing debate: in GBA1 carriers, the surgery itself does not appear to speed up dementia — delaying it does.
It offers a concrete, actionable window (within about 7-8 years of diagnosis) for referral and surgery in this higher-risk group.
It supports offering genetic testing at diagnosis to identify GBA1 carriers and plan DBS timing proactively.
It shifts DBS decisions from being based on symptoms alone toward a more personalized approach that also weighs genetics and timing.
People with Parkinson's who carry a confirmed GBA1 mutation. (GBA1 is described in the broader literature as the most common genetic risk factor in Parkinson's, occurring in roughly 8-12% of cases) (general medical guidance).
People considering DBS for tremor, stiffness, or involuntary movements (dyskinesia) that medication no longer controls reliably.
Neurologists and neurosurgeons assessing surgical candidates, since genetic status can now help inform timing.
Family members of GBA1 carriers may wish to discuss genetic counseling with a clinician, though this study looked at patients rather than relatives (general medical guidance).
If you have Parkinson's and have not had genetic testing, you may wish to ask your neurologist whether a GBA1 test is appropriate for you. (Programs such as PD GENEration, and commercial panels, offer genetic testing) (general medical guidance).
If you carry a GBA1 mutation, you may wish to ask your movement-disorder specialist about the DBS referral timeline sooner rather than later, even if symptoms are mild.
You may wish to ask about a baseline thinking (cognitive) assessment before any surgical discussion. The study used the Mattis Dementia Rating Scale; other tools such as the MoCA are also used in practice (general medical guidance).
It can help to have neurology, neurosurgery, and neuropsychology coordinate on timing, which is general good practice rather than a specific finding of this study (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • Hope: a genetic test plus well-timed surgery could help preserve years of clearer thinking for a defined group of patients.
  • Reality: many people are diagnosed more than 7 years after symptoms began, which can mean the window has already narrowed.

The Reality

  • DBS is surgery and carries general risks such as bleeding, infection, or hardware issues, and the cognitive benefit here is preventing decline rather than improving thinking (general medical guidance).
  • Access to genetic testing and to specialized DBS centers is uneven, which can affect who benefits from these findings (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

DBS remains the standard surgical therapy for advanced Parkinson's; this study refines when to use it in GBA1 carriers, not whether to use it.

The authors emphasize that carrying a GBA1 variant should not by itself disqualify someone from DBS.

For GBA1 carriers today, timely DBS is the cognitive-preservation strategy supported by this study; the cited sources do not describe other specific programs, so none are listed here.

Gene-targeted approaches for GBA1-related Parkinson's are an active research area more broadly, but were not covered by this study's sources (general medical guidance).

Evidence Uncertainty

Strengths: a large, multicenter cohort, repeated (longitudinal) thinking measures, a clear 7-8 year threshold that held at two separate cutoffs, and an effect specific to the GBA1 genotype.

Key limitation: this is an observational cohort, not a randomized trial, so it shows an association rather than proving that earlier surgery causes better thinking outcomes.

As with any observational study, healthier patients might tend to have surgery earlier, which could influence results; the paper does not specifically resolve this (general medical guidance).

It is unclear whether the same timing window applies to other genetic subtypes (such as LRRK2 or PRKN) or to newer adaptive DBS systems (general medical guidance).

What To Watch

Future professional guidance on genetic testing and DBS timing for GBA1 carriers (general medical guidance).

Longer-term follow-up of this cohort beyond its current duration.

Whether the timing effect extends to other genetic subtypes or to adaptive (closed-loop) DBS systems (general medical guidance).

Whether earlier DBS also affects underlying alpha-synuclein biology in GBA1 carriers, which would need dedicated biomarker studies (general medical guidance).
