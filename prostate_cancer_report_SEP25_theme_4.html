Focal Therapy and Active Surveillance Offer Middle Ground Between Monitoring and Radical Treatment

"Focal therapy—treating only the cancerous part of the prostate using HIFU, cryoablation, or irreversible electroporation—preserves urinary and sexual function while maintaining cancer control for carefully selected men. Combined with rigorous active surveillance protocols, this approach lets many men avoid or delay whole-gland treatment and its side effects."

Dr. Herbert Lepor reported outcomes from over 1,000 men treated with focal cryoablation [a freezing technique that destroys cancer cells]: 75% of intermediate favorable-risk patients needed no additional treatment at 7 years, with minimal incontinence and better sexual function compared to surgery or radiation.
The ProtecT trial 15-year follow-up confirmed active surveillance has the same prostate cancer mortality as immediate surgery or radiation for low-risk disease, though with a slightly higher (but still very low) metastasis rate. About half of men on surveillance eventually underwent treatment.
Northwestern Medicine developed ICE-CAP: combining cryoablation (cancer destruction) with HoLEP [holmium laser enucleation, a treatment for enlarged prostate] in a single procedure, preserving urinary and sexual function.
Sutter Health offers HIFU (ultrasound energy) and IRE (electrical pulses) as outpatient focal therapies, with treatment tailored to cancer location and patient anatomy.
Traditional choice was binary: active surveillance (anxiety, repeated biopsies) vs whole-gland surgery/radiation (high rates of incontinence, erectile dysfunction, bowel issues).
Focal therapy targets only the cancerous region (like lumpectomy for breast cancer), preserving the rest of the prostate and surrounding nerves/sphincters.
Active surveillance avoids treatment entirely for many men—ProtecT showed half of surveillance patients never needed treatment at 15 years.
ICE-CAP uniquely addresses men with BOTH prostate cancer AND bothersome urinary symptoms from enlarged prostate in a single procedure.
Men with localized, low-to-intermediate risk prostate cancer (Grade Group 1-2) confined to one prostate region.
Men with MRI-visible lesions that correlate with biopsy findings (good imaging-pathology concordance).
Men who prioritize preserving urinary continence and sexual function over absolute maximal cancer control.
Men with enlarged prostates and urinary obstruction who also have localized cancer (ICE-CAP candidates).
Men willing to commit to rigorous long-term surveillance (PSA, MRI, biopsies) after focal therapy.
Ask your urologist: "Am I a candidate for active surveillance or focal therapy based on my MRI and biopsy results?"
Request MRI-targeted biopsy with systematic sampling to accurately map cancer location—this determines focal therapy eligibility.
Seek a center with dedicated focal therapy experience and longitudinal outcome tracking (ask about their 5-7 year data).
If you have both cancer and urinary symptoms, ask about combined procedures like ICE-CAP that address both in one surgery.

The Hope

  • Focal therapy is not "cancer cure without consequences"—15-25% need additional treatment (salvage surgery/radiation or repeat focal) within 7-10 years.
  • Active surveillance requires discipline: missed MRIs/biopsies risk missing progression; anxiety about "living with cancer" is real for many men.

The Reality

  • Insurance coverage for focal therapy varies; some plans may consider certain modalities investigational. Out-of-pocket costs can be substantial (general medical guidance).
  • Not all urologists offer or support focal therapy—you may need to travel to a specialized center, adding logistical and financial burden.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

HIFU (High-Intensity Focused Ultrasound) [uses focused sound waves to heat and destroy cancer tissue]: Outpatient, no incision, MRI-ultrasound fusion guidance (general medical guidance).

IRE (Irreversible Electroporation) [uses electrical pulses to destroy cancer cells without heat]: Needle-based, precise margins; outpatient (general medical guidance).

Focal Cryoablation: Freeze-thaw cycles destroy tissue; real-time ultrasound monitoring; Dr. Lepor's protocol: 6-month biopsy, then PSA q6mo + MRI at intervals.

ICE-CAP: Cryoablation + HoLEP (holmium laser enucleation) same session; for men with cancer + obstructive BPH; single anesthetic, single recovery.

Evidence Uncertainty

Focal therapy: Strongest data from single-institution prospective registries (Lepor: 1,000+ patients, 7-year follow-up for intermediate favorable-risk); no randomized trials vs. radical treatment; selection bias likely (general medical guidance).

ProtecT trial: Highest level of evidence for active surveillance safety in low-risk disease; about half of surveillance patients eventually underwent treatment at 15 years.

ICE-CAP: Early case series only; no long-term oncologic or functional outcomes published yet.

Focal therapy modalities lack head-to-head comparisons and standardized patient selection criteria across centers (general medical guidance).

What To Watch

Results from randomized trials comparing focal therapy to active surveillance or radical treatment (general medical guidance).

Standardization of focal therapy patient selection criteria, treatment protocols, and follow-up schedules across institutions.

Long-term (15+ year) metastasis-free and overall survival data from prospective focal therapy registries.

Improved imaging (MRI, PSMA-PET) for better patient selection and post-treatment monitoring (general medical guidance).

Cost-effectiveness analyses comparing lifetime costs of focal therapy + surveillance vs radical treatment + side effect management.
