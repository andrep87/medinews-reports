CGM and Automated Insulin Delivery: Expanding Benefits Beyond Insulin Users

"New research shows continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) help adults with type 2 diabetes not on insulin spend about five more hours daily in a healthy glucose range and lower A1c by nearly 1 percentage point. At the same time, automated insulin delivery (AID) systems are now considered the preferred option for type 1 diabetes, though smart insulin pens remain a valid choice for some."

The CONNECT trial tested real-time CGM in 283 adults with type 2 diabetes not using insulin across 22 U.S. primary care clinics. After 26 weeks, CGM users spent 21 percentage points more time in the target glucose range (70–180 mg/dL) — about 5 extra hours per day — and lowered A1c by 0.9 points compared to routine care.
The EBIACE-1 crossover trial compared a smart insulin pen (InPen) with an automated insulin delivery system (MiniMed 780G) in 31 adults with type 1 diabetes. AID delivered 81% time in range vs. 66% with the smart pen, and A1c was 6.9% vs. 7.3%.
The FOX Study exposed 30 adults (15 with diabetes) to low-oxygen conditions (14.5% oxygen) while wearing Dexcom G6 and FreeStyle Libre sensors. Both systems lost accuracy during hypoxia, especially when glucose was changing rapidly; Dexcom G6 was more affected.
An AI-supported diabetic retinopathy [diabetes-related damage to the back of the eye] screening program in Tanzania roughly doubled specialist follow-up (35.9% vs 18.2%) and cut wait times from 37 days to 11 days by giving immediate results and in-person counseling at the point of care.
CGM gives people not on insulin a powerful new tool: seeing glucose trends in real time helps them link food, activity, and stress to glucose changes, leading to behavior changes that lower A1c without new medications.
For type 1 diabetes, AID systems automate basal insulin adjustments every few minutes, reducing the mental burden of constant dosing decisions. The 2026 ADA/EASD consensus positions AID as the preferred method of insulin delivery for most people who can use it consistently (general medical guidance).
CGM accuracy drops in low-oxygen settings (high altitude, air travel). Knowing this prevents dangerous treatment decisions based on faulty readings — like taking extra insulin for a falsely high value.
AI screening closes a critical gap: many people with diabetic eye disease never see a specialist. Immediate results and counseling at the screening visit turn a "referral" into an actual appointment, preventing vision loss.
Adults with type 2 diabetes not on insulin who have A1c ≥7.5% and want to understand their glucose patterns — especially those on GLP-1 agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, or multiple oral medications.
Adults with type 1 diabetes considering technology options: AID is now first-line for most, but smart pens are a strong alternative for those who prefer injections, have pump-site issues, or face cost/access barriers.
Anyone with diabetes who travels to high altitude, flies frequently, or exercises at elevation — CGM readings may be less reliable during and after these exposures.
People with diabetes in underserved areas where specialist access is limited — AI-supported screening can bring eye care to primary care settings.
You may wish to ask your primary care provider whether a CGM trial is appropriate if your A1c is above target and you are not on insulin; some insurers cover short-term professional CGM for treatment optimization (general medical guidance).
If you have type 1 diabetes on multiple daily injections, you may wish to ask whether an AID system could reduce your daily burden, and to discuss smart pens if you want dose tracking without a pump (general medical guidance).
Before high-altitude travel or flying, you may wish to pack a fingerstick meter, confirm symptoms with a fingerstick rather than relying on CGM alone, and discuss sick-day rules with your team (general medical guidance).
If offered AI-based eye screening, you may wish to ask for the result and explanation at the visit and, if referred, to schedule the specialist appointment before leaving the clinic (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • CGM and AID are transformative but not magic: they require wearing a device, responding to alerts, and maintaining skin sites. Alarm fatigue and cost (sensors, transmitters, pumps) are real barriers.
  • AID reduces but doesn't eliminate daily tasks: you still count carbs, announce meals, change infusion sets, troubleshoot sensors, and manage ketones. "Hands-off" is a myth.

The Reality

  • Smart pens are cheaper and simpler but don't automate basal insulin. For some, they hit the sweet spot of data + freedom. Don't feel pressured to "upgrade" if you're meeting goals with MDI.
  • AI screening finds disease earlier but doesn't treat it. You still need access to laser, injections, or surgery. In low-resource areas, the screening-treatment gap remains.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Real-time CGM (examples include Dexcom and FreeStyle Libre systems): sensors worn about 10-14 days that transmit glucose every few minutes to a phone or receiver; used by both insulin and non-insulin users, with CONNECT strengthening the non-insulin evidence (general medical guidance).

Automated insulin delivery (AID): systems that combine a CGM, an insulin pump, and an algorithm to adjust basal insulin automatically while the user still announces meals; the 2026 ADA/EASD consensus positions AID as preferred for type 1 diabetes (general medical guidance).

Smart insulin pens: Bluetooth-enabled pens that log doses, calculate boluses, show insulin-on-board, and can sync with a CGM; they do not automate delivery and suit people on multiple daily injections who want dose data (general medical guidance).

AI retinopathy screening: software that analyzes retinal photos at the point of care and flags people who need a specialist referral; shown to improve follow-up in the Tanzania trial (general medical guidance).

Evidence Uncertainty

CONNECT trial: 283 participants, 26 weeks, primary care setting — high generalizability. Primary outcomes: time in range (+21%, p<0.001) and A1c (-0.9%, p<0.001). No severe hypoglycemia. Limitation: 31% in CGM group added medications vs. 17% control; behavioral effect likely but not isolated.

EBIACE-1: 31 adults, crossover design (each person tried both). Strong within-person comparison. AID superior on averages, but 46% achieved near-optimal control with smart pen. Small sample; only two devices tested.

FOX Study: 30 adults in controlled low-oxygen conditions. Accuracy (measured as MARD — mean absolute relative difference; lower is better) worsened during hypoxia, more so during rapid glucose changes. Lab conditions do not fully replicate real-world altitude (temperature, pressure, and activity were not reproduced).

AI Screening Trial: 2,378 participants, 24 hospitals, cluster-randomized. Follow-up at 8 weeks: 35.9% vs. 18.2% (p<0.001). Immediate results + counseling key. Limitation: Tanzania setting; specialist access still needed for treatment.

What To Watch

ADA/EASD guideline update: CONNECT trial data may upgrade CGM recommendation for non-insulin type 2 diabetes from Level C to Level A evidence, expanding insurance coverage.

Next-gen AID: fully closed-loop (no meal announcement), integration with GLP-1 agonists, smaller pumps, longer sensor wear (21+ days).

CGM in special populations: pregnancy, CKD, hospitalized patients, and real-world high-altitude performance studies.

AI expansion: multi-disease screening (retinopathy + nephropathy + neuropathy from single retinal image), home-based screening via smartphone.

Omnipod Discover and similar platforms: real-world data on whether HCP dashboard access improves time in range and reduces clinic visits.
