New Alzheimer's Treatments Move Beyond Amyloid to Tau and Brain Delivery

"New Alzheimer's drugs are moving beyond amyloid plaques to target tau protein and brain immune cells. These emerging approaches may eventually help patients who cannot use current treatments or who need additional options — but cognitive benefit in patients has not yet been shown, and most are in early human testing."

Arrowhead's ARO-MAPT, an RNA interference therapy [a drug that lowers production of a specific protein by silencing its gene], reduced tau in the cerebrospinal fluid of healthy volunteers by a mean of 73% at day 105 (with a maximum individual reduction of 85%), using a subcutaneous (under-the-skin) injection designed to reach the brain (independent fact-check: the draft led with '85%'; the headline result is the 73% mean).
A small-molecule drug called VG-3927, an oral TREM2 agonist [a pill that activates a receptor to boost the brain's immune cells, called microglia], reduced a key microglial biomarker (sTREM2) by up to about 50% in cerebrospinal fluid and was generally well tolerated in a Phase 1 trial of roughly 80 healthy volunteers (about 60 of whom received the drug).
Researchers developed "molecular glue degraders" that reduced amyloid precursor proteins before they could form plaques, in mouse models.
FDA-approved drugs lecanemab (Leqembi) and donanemab (Kisunla) already modestly slow progression by removing amyloid plaques in early Alzheimer's.
Current amyloid treatments do not work for everyone and carry risks such as brain swelling or small bleeds (general medical guidance).
Tau-targeting drugs address a different brain change closely linked to cognitive decline, which could eventually broaden who can be treated — though patient benefit has not yet been shown.
Oral or injectable small molecules could be easier to give than IV infusions and may reach the brain effectively (general medical guidance).
New candidates expand the treatment pipeline beyond the two currently approved disease-modifying drugs.
People with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to Alzheimer's who may qualify for current amyloid drugs (general medical guidance).
Patients who cannot take amyloid drugs (for example, due to blood thinners or high risk of brain swelling) who may be interested in future alternatives (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • New tau and microglia drugs offer hope for patients who cannot use current amyloid therapies, but most are years from potential approval (general medical guidance).
  • Current disease-modifying drugs require frequent infusions and MRI monitoring, and their cost and coverage can be significant barriers (general medical guidance).

The Reality

  • Benefits of approved drugs are measured in months of slowed decline, not cures or restoration of lost memory (general medical guidance).
  • Insurance coverage and reimbursement for newer drugs remain uncertain in many regions (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Lecanemab (Leqembi, Eisai): an FDA-approved anti-amyloid antibody, given by IV (every two weeks under its standard schedule) (general medical guidance).

Donanemab (Kisunla, Eli Lilly): an FDA-approved anti-amyloid antibody, given by IV (monthly under its standard schedule) (general medical guidance).

ARO-MAPT (Arrowhead): an RNA interference therapy that lowers tau production; in Phase 1/2a testing (healthy volunteers, with an early Alzheimer's cohort planned).

VG-3927 (Vigil Neuroscience): an oral small-molecule TREM2 agonist that boosts microglial activity; Phase 1 complete, with a Phase 2 planned (independent fact-check: the draft listed the developer as 'Viridis/Vialee' and '115 participants'; the trial enrolled about 80, developed by Vigil Neuroscience).

Evidence Uncertainty

ARO-MAPT achieved a mean 73% reduction (85% maximum) in cerebrospinal fluid tau at day 105 in healthy volunteers, but has not yet been tested in people with Alzheimer's; it is a Phase 1/2a program, not Phase 2/3.

VG-3927 was generally well tolerated and reduced sTREM2, but cognitive benefit in patients has not been shown.

Lecanemab and donanemab modestly slow cognitive decline but do not stop or reverse the disease; brain swelling or bleeding occurs in some patients (general medical guidance).

Molecular glue degraders reduced plaque-forming proteins in mice; human trials have not yet begun.

What To Watch

Phase 1/2a results for ARO-MAPT in people with early Alzheimer's disease (the planned patient cohort).

Long-term outcomes for patients receiving lecanemab and donanemab through real-world registries (general medical guidance).

Progress of molecular glue degraders toward human clinical trials.

New oral or subcutaneous options that could reduce treatment burden compared with IV infusions (general medical guidance).
