Blood Tests Are Changing How Alzheimer's Is Diagnosed and Monitored

"Blood tests can now detect Alzheimer's-related brain changes, in some settings years before symptoms appear, and help track how patients respond to amyloid-clearing drugs. Moving from spinal taps and PET scans toward a simple blood draw could support earlier diagnosis and more personalized monitoring, though these tests are not yet routine in everyday primary care and access barriers remain."

A real-world study of 197 patients taking lecanemab (Leqembi, an amyloid-clearing antibody) found that 34 proteins measured in blood (plasma) changed in four distinct patterns during treatment: some moved back toward healthy levels, some overshot, some moved opposite to what was expected, and some did not change.
In people with Down syndrome (who are at very high lifetime risk of Alzheimer's), a synaptic protein called β-synuclein [a marker of the connections between brain cells] rose in the blood about 21 years before early symptoms — earlier than markers of amyloid, tau, or nerve-cell damage.
An FDA-cleared p-tau217 blood test [which measures a tau-related protein tied to Alzheimer's] helped rule out Alzheimer's with about 97% negative predictive value and rule it in with about 92% positive predictive value against PET or spinal-fluid testing; serious kidney failure and severe obesity can raise the marker and cause false positives.
China's regulator (NMPA) approved ultra-sensitive SIMOA blood tests [a lab method that detects proteins at very low levels] for four brain markers (p-tau217, GFAP, p-tau181, and NfL), expanding access to high-precision testing in that market.
Researchers are also studying whether tear fluid and RNA carried in tiny blood particles could allow even earlier, less invasive screening, including in lower-resource settings.
A blood draw is routine and repeatable, unlike a lumbar puncture (spinal tap) or an amyloid PET scan, which are more invasive or costly and often limited to specialty centers — so blood testing points toward wider, earlier diagnosis, even though it is not yet standard in primary care.
The different biomarker patterns seen during lecanemab treatment may, with more research, help answer practical questions such as whether the drug is clearing amyloid — though researchers caution these patterns are not yet ready to change who gets treated or when.
β-synuclein's early rise in Down syndrome suggests damage to brain-cell connections can begin decades before memory symptoms, which may open a window for earlier monitoring or trial enrollment.
For patients, the overall direction is toward faster diagnosis, fewer invasive procedures, and monitoring that could eventually be tailored to a person's own biology.
People with memory concerns or a family history: blood tests such as p-tau217 are emerging as an early option to help rule Alzheimer's pathology in or out before confirmatory testing.
People already taking lecanemab: emerging biomarker patterns (p-tau217, NfL, GFAP) are being studied as a way to monitor treatment response. Researchers note these do not yet guide treatment decisions, and this evidence currently applies to lecanemab, not other drugs.
Adults with Down syndrome: tracking β-synuclein could help identify people for prevention trials and earlier monitoring, potentially starting in the 30s rather than the 50s.
People in underserved or rural areas: blood-based testing, and tear or RNA approaches still in development, could lower barriers to specialist-level diagnosis.
People with kidney disease or obesity: a p-tau217 result should be interpreted alongside kidney function (eGFR) and weight, because these conditions can raise the marker without Alzheimer's being present.
You may wish to ask your primary care doctor or neurologist whether a p-tau217 blood test is appropriate to help clarify whether memory changes are related to Alzheimer's. The test is FDA-cleared; insurance coverage varies.
If you take lecanemab, you may wish to ask whether your care team tracks plasma markers such as p-tau217, NfL, or GFAP over time, and how those trends might inform the timing of a future amyloid PET scan.
If you have Down syndrome, or care for someone who does, you may wish to ask a neurologist about studies tracking β-synuclein or p-tau217, which may open access to early-intervention research.
You may wish to ask that kidney function (eGFR) and weight be documented alongside any Alzheimer's blood test, since this can help avoid a false-positive result from kidney-related elevation of p-tau217.
You may wish to ask about joining a research registry so you can be contacted about biomarker studies, many of which need participants from diverse backgrounds to validate these tests (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • Hope: a simple blood draw could eventually replace spinal taps and PET scans for many people, make monitoring more personal, and help prevention trials start earlier.
  • Reality: these tests are not yet routine in primary care, and many clinicians are still learning when to order and how to interpret them (inference from the cited accuracy and confounder data).
  • Access: wider rollout depends on validation across diverse populations; one key cohort was 98% White, which limits how far the results generalize.

The Reality

  • Confounders are real: kidney disease and severe obesity can raise p-tau217 and require careful clinical interpretation rather than a test result alone.
  • Not a cure: even accurate biomarkers only support diagnosis and monitoring; they do not themselves slow or reverse the disease.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Lecanemab (Leqembi): an anti-amyloid antibody studied here in a 197-patient longitudinal cohort for its effect on blood biomarkers over about two years.

p-tau217 blood test: an FDA-cleared test used as a diagnostic aid, reported at about 97% negative predictive value and 92% positive predictive value against PET or spinal-fluid testing.

SIMOA platform (Quanterix): an ultra-sensitive method that detects markers at very low (single-molecule) levels, now approved in China for p-tau217, GFAP, p-tau181, and NfL.

Earlier-stage approaches: β-synuclein (a marker of synaptic health), RNA carried in tiny blood particles, and tear-fluid markers are all still in validation for pre-symptom detection and monitoring (general medical guidance).

Evidence Uncertainty

Strongest evidence: p-tau217 accuracy of about 97% negative and 92% positive predictive value against PET/spinal-fluid standards; the lecanemab biomarker study (n=197, up to two years) showed reproducible biomarker trajectories.

β-synuclein in Down syndrome: cross-sectional (n=131), with roughly 1.8-fold to 3.7-fold higher levels across disease stages and a rise preceding other markers by more than seven years; longitudinal confirmation is still needed.

Key limitations: many biomarker studies were done in largely White cohorts (one cohort was 98% White); kidney disease and obesity can confound p-tau217; tear and RNA approaches are still pre-diagnostic; and the SIMOA tests' approval so far is in China, so their routine US status is not addressed in these sources.

Still unknown: whether changing treatment based on biomarkers (for example, pausing lecanemab when amyloid appears cleared) preserves benefit, and how closely blood-marker changes track long-term thinking and memory. One researcher notes these findings should not, at this stage, exclude anyone from therapy.

What To Watch

Longer-term biomarker studies in people taking lecanemab, including whether marker trends can flag ARIA [amyloid-related imaging abnormalities — brain swelling or small bleeds] before symptoms appear, which researchers are now investigating.

Longitudinal β-synuclein studies in Down syndrome and the general population to see whether it predicts progression in individuals.

Whether combining several markers (such as p-tau217, the Aβ42/40 ratio, NfL, GFAP, and β-synuclein) improves staging (general medical guidance).

Global access questions, including the Quanterix/Innovita rollout in China and the UNC tear-biomarker study in Peru, and whether low-cost blood tests reach lower-resource settings.

How quickly blood-based testing moves from specialty centers into routine primary care (general medical guidance).
