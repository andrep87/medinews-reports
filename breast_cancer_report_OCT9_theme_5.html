Exercise May Slow Biological Aging Markers in Breast Cancer Survivors

"A secondary analysis of a randomized trial found that a 12-month home-based exercise program was associated with slower epigenetic aging, measured by DNA methylation clocks, in 124 breast cancer survivors compared with a wellness education control group. Slower biological aging correlated with better attention scores. This was a secondary analysis of a small trial, and the findings are preliminary."

Secondary analysis of the I Can! RCT: 124 inactive breast cancer survivors (stage I–III, >40 years, 6+ months post-treatment) randomized to 12-month remote exercise program (Fitbit + coaching calls/emails) vs health/wellness education control.
Exercise group increased moderate-vigorous activity from 16.7 to 22.6 min/day at 6 months; control group stayed near baseline (12 to 13.5 min/day).
Epigenetic aging (GrimAge2, DNAmFitAge clocks) slowed significantly in exercise group: P=0.02 and P=0.002 respectively.
Biological-chronological age gap narrowed by 0.92 years in exercisers vs 0.19 years in controls at 12 months.
Cancer treatment accelerates biological aging (epigenetic age exceeded chronological by ~8.7 years at baseline); exercise may partially reverse this.
Faster epigenetic aging correlated with worse attention (GrimAge2, r=-0.20) and self-reported cognition (DNAmFitAge, r=-0.19); exercise attenuated these associations.
Exercise-induced methylation changes enriched in BDNF signaling pathway (VAV2, GABRB3, CRTC1 genes)—plausible link to neuroplasticity and cognitive resilience.
The methylation changes observed were enriched in a pathway related to brain-derived neurotrophic factor, which the researchers describe as a mechanistically plausible hypothesis linking exercise to cognitive resilience — not established mechanism (audit correction).
Breast cancer survivors (any stage I–III) within 5 years of diagnosis, post-active treatment.
Particularly relevant for survivors reporting "chemo brain," fatigue, or accelerated aging concerns.
Benefit seen with moderate activity increase (~6 min/day more MVPA)—achievable for most survivors.
Not limited to young survivors; mean age 58.8 years in trial.
Regular physical activity has well-established benefits for cancer survivors. You may wish to discuss with your care team what level of activity is appropriate for you (general medical guidance).
In this trial, the exercise group increased moderate-to-vigorous activity by roughly 6 minutes per day — a modest change that was achievable for most participants.
You may wish to ask your oncology team about referral to a cancer rehabilitation or exercise oncology program if one is available (general medical guidance).
If you are experiencing memory, concentration, or word-finding difficulties, raising this at follow-up visits is reasonable (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • Hope: Exercise is low-cost, widely accessible, and has multiple established benefits for survivors. The remote coaching model used here worked without requiring in-person visits.
  • Reality: Sustained behavior change is difficult, and adherence is challenging without ongoing support.

The Reality

  • Exercise does not substitute for medical surveillance, endocrine therapy, screening, or any indicated treatment.
  • Describing this as 'reversing aging' would overstate the finding. Epigenetic clocks are biomarkers, and this analysis cannot establish effects on dementia, recurrence, or survival.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

The I Can! trial used a remote exercise program combining a wearable activity tracker with coaching calls and emails over 12 months.

Epigenetic clocks (GrimAge2, DNAmFitAge) are research tools that estimate biological age from DNA methylation patterns. They are biomarkers, not direct measures of health or lifespan.

The methylation changes observed were enriched in a brain-derived neurotrophic factor signaling pathway, which the researchers identify as a possible mechanism requiring further study.

Digital health tools including apps, wearables, and telehealth coaching are expanding access to structured exercise programs (general medical guidance).

Evidence Uncertainty

Strengths: RCT design; objective accelerometer data; multiple epigenetic clocks; genome-wide methylation; cognitive assessments.

Limitations: This was a secondary analysis rather than the trial's primary endpoint. With 124 participants it was underpowered for subgroup analysis, and the population was predominantly White and highly educated.

Not all epigenetic clocks changed (Horvath, Hannum, PhenoAge, telomere length unchanged)—effect specific to GrimAge2/DNAmFitAge.

Cannot conclude slowed epigenetic aging causes reduced dementia, CVD, recurrence, or mortality—requires larger, longer trials.

What To Watch

Larger confirmatory trials with cognitive and clinical endpoints.

Whether the association between slower epigenetic aging and better attention holds up in bigger studies.

Further work on the brain-derived neurotrophic factor pathway changes observed here.

Whether these findings replicate in more diverse survivor populations (general medical guidance).
