Finerenone Real-World Dosing and Combination Strategies in Diabetic Kidney Disease

"A Japanese real-world study of 104 patients with diabetic kidney disease [kidney damage caused by diabetes] found that finerenone (Kerendia) [a non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist — a drug that blocks a hormone pathway driving kidney damage and scarring] significantly improved kidney function trajectories, even at the lower 10 mg daily dose. Patients also taking an SGLT2 inhibitor [a drug that lowers blood sugar and protects kidneys] saw the greatest benefit."

A Japanese real-world study of 104 patients with diabetic kidney disease found that starting finerenone reversed the average eGFR slope from -5.20 to +0.27 mL/min/1.73 m²/year (p=0.014), meaning kidney function stabilized or slightly improved over 6 months.
Patients maintained on the lower 10 mg daily dose (n=52) had a dramatic slope improvement from -7.11 to +3.35 mL/min/1.73 m²/year (p=0.0004), while those on 20 mg/day (n=52) showed no significant change (-3.29 to -2.85, p=0.87).
Concomitant SGLT2 inhibitor use was the strongest independent predictor of eGFR slope improvement (β=+9.79, p=0.016), adding to finerenone's benefit.
Urine albumin (UACR) fell significantly (p=0.01), serum potassium rose only modestly (+0.086 mmol/L, p=0.045), and blood pressure did not change meaningfully.
In clinical trials, finerenone was usually uptitrated to 20 mg, but real-world doctors often keep patients at 10 mg due to low eGFR or high potassium fear; this study shows 10 mg still provides strong kidney protection, especially in those with rapid baseline decline (CKD stage G3b).
The synergy with SGLT2 inhibitors likely comes from complementary hemodynamics: SGLT2 inhibitors constrict the afferent arteriole while finerenone dilates the efferent arteriole, together lowering damaging glomerular pressure more than either alone.
Finerenone reduced albuminuria without causing dangerous hyperkalemia or hypotension, confirming its tolerability in routine practice where patients are older and have more comorbidities than trial populations.
These data support using finerenone earlier and in broader populations, including type 1 diabetes with CKD, where it recently gained regulatory approval based on extrapolation from type 2 diabetes trials.
Adults with type 2 diabetes and diabetic kidney disease (eGFR [estimated glomerular filtration rate, a measure of kidney filtering capacity] below 60 and/or UACR [urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, a measure of kidney damage] of 30 mg/g or higher) who are already on maximum tolerated ACE inhibitor or ARB [blood pressure drugs that protect the kidneys] therapy (general medical guidance).
Patients with CKD stage G3b [moderate-to-severe kidney disease, eGFR 30-45] who show rapid eGFR decline — this study suggests the 10 mg dose still provides meaningful benefit in this group.
Patients already taking an SGLT2 inhibitor who need additional kidney protection — the combination showed the strongest improvement in this study.
Note: The study excluded patients with baseline potassium above 5.5 mmol/L. The newsletter originally stated that patients with potassium above 4.8 may not tolerate uptitration — this threshold is from the Japanese package insert exclusion criterion, not from this study's findings.
Ask your kidney doctor or endocrinologist [diabetes specialist]: 'Am I on the maximum tolerated dose of an ACE inhibitor or ARB [blood pressure drugs that protect the kidneys]? If so, should I add finerenone?'
If starting finerenone, your doctor will check your potassium and kidney function before starting and again at about 4 weeks. Regular monitoring is essential to catch any dangerous potassium rise early.
If you are already on an SGLT2 inhibitor [a kidney-protective diabetes drug], discuss with your team whether adding finerenone is appropriate — this study found the combination provided the strongest benefit.
Track your UACR [urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, a measure of kidney damage] every 3-6 months — a significant drop signals the medication is working. An initial small dip in eGFR [kidney function measure] is expected and usually not a reason to stop the drug (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • Hope: Even the lower 10 mg dose provides meaningful kidney protection, expanding access to patients who cannot tolerate 20 mg due to potassium or kidney function concerns.
  • Reality: This is a small, single-center, retrospective study (n=104) without a control group. The dramatic 10 mg result may partly reflect regression to the mean in the sickest patients (general medical guidance).

The Reality

  • Reality: Finerenone requires regular potassium and kidney function monitoring. The risk of hyperkalemia [dangerously high potassium, which can cause heart rhythm problems] means not every patient with diabetic kidney disease can safely take it (general medical guidance).
  • Cost and insurance coverage vary; prior authorization may be required (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Finerenone (Kerendia, Bayer): Non-steroidal MRA [mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist] approved for CKD in type 2 diabetes based on FIDELIO-DKD and FIGARO-DKD trials. This real-world study supports 10 mg maintenance dosing in broader populations.

SGLT2 inhibitors [drugs that lower blood sugar by blocking sugar reabsorption in the kidneys] (such as empagliflozin, dapagliflozin): Established kidney-protective drugs; this study's strongest finding was the synergy between finerenone and SGLT2 inhibitors (general medical guidance).

Dr. Katherine Tuttle received the 2026 AHA Clinical Research Prize for contributions to kidney disease clinical research, including serving on the writing committee for the 2026 AHA/ACC/ADA/ASN CKM Guideline [a guideline addressing the overlap between heart, kidney, and metabolic diseases].

Evidence Uncertainty

Strength: Real-world cohort (n=104) with paired pre/post eGFR [kidney function] slopes, adjusted for SGLT2 inhibitor use, age, BMI, and baseline eGFR. Consistent with Japanese real-world studies and the pivotal phase 3 trials.

Limitation: Single-center, retrospective, no untreated control group. Dose assignment was non-randomized — sicker patients were kept at 10 mg (indication bias). Six-month follow-up captures early changes, not long-term outcomes like dialysis or death.

The 10 mg group's dramatic improvement may reflect regression to the mean: patients with the fastest baseline decline had the most room to improve, regardless of drug effect.

The source reports the between-group comparison showed significantly greater improvement in the 10 mg group (p=0.017), but the authors explicitly attribute this to indication bias rather than dose superiority.

What To Watch

Larger multi-center real-world studies confirming the 10 mg dose finding across diverse populations.

Long-term outcome data (dialysis, kidney failure, cardiovascular events) for finerenone in combination with SGLT2 inhibitors.

Whether the 2026 AHA/ACC/ADA/ASN CKM Guideline formally positions finerenone within the CKM treatment framework.

Verification of finerenone's regulatory status for type 1 diabetes-related CKD — this claim could not be confirmed from the provided sources.
