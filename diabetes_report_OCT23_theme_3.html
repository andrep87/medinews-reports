Automated Insulin Delivery (AID) Systems Now Cleared for Type 2 Diabetes

"The FDA has cleared the first automated insulin delivery (AID) system—the Sequel twiist—for adults with type 2 diabetes on insulin. In a pivotal trial, pump-naïve participants achieved a 0.7% A1C reduction and gained 3.8 more hours per day in target glucose range (70–180 mg/dL) within 13 weeks, with no severe hypoglycemia."

In October 2026, the FDA cleared the twiist automated insulin delivery (AID) system [an "artificial pancreas" — an insulin pump that automatically adjusts insulin based on CGM readings] for adults 18 and older with insulin-treated type 2 diabetes, expanding from its earlier type 1 diabetes clearance (independent fact-check: the original type 1 clearance was in 2024, with the national launch in 2026; the draft's 'March 2026' referred to the launch).
The pivotal trial enrolled 307 adults across 30 U.S. sites; only 11% had prior carb-counting experience and 19% had prior AID experience, so most were new to pump therapy.
Average A1C fell from 8.1% to 7.4% (a 0.7-point reduction); participants who started with an A1C of 9% or higher saw about a 2.1-point reduction. Time in the target range (70-180 mg/dL) rose from 57% to 73% (about 3.8 more hours per day).
Participants switching from multiple daily injections used about 19.3 fewer insulin units per day on average, and no severe low blood sugar occurred during the trial.
AID systems were historically approved only for type 1 diabetes; this clearance brings automated insulin dosing to the much larger group of people with insulin-treated type 2 diabetes (estimated around 5.5 million U.S. adults).
The system's algorithm adjusts background insulin every few minutes based on CGM trends, correcting highs and easing off before lows — reducing the constant mental math of dosing (general medical guidance).
Many participants improved quickly even without prior pump or carb-counting experience, suggesting a relatively gentle learning curve.
The system works with CGMs that many people with type 2 diabetes already wear (such as the FreeStyle Libre 3 Plus and Eversense 365).
Adults 18 and older with type 2 diabetes on any insulin regimen who are not at their glucose target (general medical guidance).
People willing to wear an insulin pump and a compatible CGM continuously (general medical guidance).
People who struggle with glucose swings or the burden of multiple daily injections, even if they have never used a pump (general medical guidance).
Likely not suitable for people unwilling to wear a pump/CGM, or those with severe hypoglycemia unawareness without caregiver support (general medical guidance).

You may wish to confirm CGM compatibility; if you use a sensor the system supports, you can likely keep it (general medical guidance).
It helps to request a "pump start" appointment with a certified diabetes care and education specialist trained on the system (general medical guidance).
Before starting, you may wish to review the key settings (such as glucose targets and meal features) with your care team to fit your routine (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • AID reduces but does not eliminate diabetes self-management: users still estimate carbs, change infusion sites, maintain the CGM, and respond to alerts (general medical guidance).
  • Cost and coverage vary: pump hardware and ongoing supplies are expensive, and some plans still apply type-1-focused criteria (general medical guidance).

The Reality

  • Technology issues can occur (pump occlusions, CGM signal loss, site reactions), so a backup plan (pen injections, ketone strips) and support access matter (general medical guidance).
  • Average A1C fell to 7.4%, still above goal for some; additional therapy (such as GLP-1, SGLT2, or mealtime insulin adjustments) may be needed (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

twiist AID system (Sequel Med Tech): a tubed pump using the Tidepool Loop algorithm; cleared for type 1 diabetes (ages 6+) and now type 2 diabetes (adults 18+) (general medical guidance).

Other AID systems are being studied or sought for type 2 diabetes use (general medical guidance).

All current AID systems are "hybrid closed-loop": they automate background insulin, but meal doses still require user input (general medical guidance).

Future directions include fully closed-loop systems (no meal announcement) and integration with newer insulins (general medical guidance).

Evidence Uncertainty

The pivotal trial was a 13-week, single-arm study (no control group) of 307 participants across 30 sites, with a diverse population and most participants continuing modern diabetes medications (independent fact-check: in the trial, about 56% were on a GLP-1 receptor agonist and about 37% on an SGLT2 inhibitor; the draft's '16% on both' figure has been removed as unverified).

Strengths: a real-world population, high use of automated mode, rapid improvement, no severe low blood sugar, and improved patient-reported satisfaction and sleep.

Limitations: no randomized comparator, short duration (13 weeks), and manufacturer funding.

Longer-term data and post-market monitoring will be important for durability and device performance (general medical guidance).
