Continuous Glucose Monitoring Proven Effective for Type 2 Diabetes Not on Insulin

"A landmark clinical trial published in the New England Journal of Medicine shows that continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) significantly improve blood sugar control in adults with type 2 diabetes who do not take insulin. CGM users spent about five more hours per day in their target glucose range and were nearly twice as likely to reach an A1C below 7% compared to those using finger-stick testing alone."

The CONNECT trial randomly assigned 283 adults with type 2 diabetes not using insulin to either a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) [a small wearable sensor that checks blood sugar automatically every few minutes] or standard finger-stick monitoring for 26 weeks, across 22 U.S. primary care practices.
CGM users lowered their A1C [a blood test showing average blood sugar over about 3 months] by about 1.6 percentage points (from 8.8% to 7.2%), versus about 0.7 points in the finger-stick group — a difference of roughly 0.9 points, which is clinically meaningful.
CGM users spent about 21% more time in the target glucose range (70-180 mg/dL) — roughly five extra hours per day — with no increase in low blood sugar (hypoglycemia).
About half of CGM users reached an A1C below 7% versus about one-quarter of the finger-stick group, and the benefit was consistent across ages, races, and medication regimens, including SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists (general medical guidance).
Millions of U.S. adults have type 2 diabetes and do not take insulin, yet many do not reach their blood-sugar targets; this trial offers strong evidence that CGM can help close that gap.
Current ADA guidelines give their highest evidence rating for CGM in insulin-treated patients but a lower rating for non-insulin users; results like these may support upgrading that recommendation and expanding insurance coverage (general medical guidance).
Real-time glucose feedback helps people see how food, activity, stress, and sleep affect their blood sugar right away, supporting changes that occasional finger sticks cannot.
The trial was done in primary care, where most type 2 diabetes is managed, so the findings apply directly to everyday practice.

The Hope

  • A CGM is a tool, not a treatment — it reveals patterns but does not lower glucose by itself; the benefit comes from acting on the data (adjusting meals, walking after eating, timing medications).
  • Cost can be a barrier: without insurance, CGM systems can be expensive, and coverage for non-insulin users varies by plan (general medical guidance).

The Reality

  • Some users experience sensor-adhesion issues, skin reactions, or alarm fatigue, and a portion discontinue within the first year (general medical guidance).
  • An A1C improvement of around 0.9 points is meaningful but does not cure diabetes; ongoing lifestyle effort and medication adherence remain essential (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Dexcom G7: a factory-calibrated, ~10-day wearable sensor with real-time phone alerts; the brand tested in CONNECT (general medical guidance).

Abbott FreeStyle Libre 3: a ~14-day sensor with optional real-time alarms; coverage for non-insulin users is expanding (general medical guidance).

Eversense 365 (Senseonics): an implantable sensor placed by a clinician that lasts up to a year, for people who prefer not to change sensors often (general medical guidance).

A planned extension of the CONNECT trial is expected to assess whether the A1C and time-in-range benefits last at 12 months.

Evidence Uncertainty

Strengths: a randomized controlled trial (283 participants) in real-world primary care, with a diverse population reflecting contemporary practice (including SGLT2 inhibitor and incretin use).

The main endpoint was change in A1C at 26 weeks, which was met with statistical significance; time in range also improved significantly.

Limitations: the trial was funded by the CGM manufacturer (Dexcom) and was open-label (participants knew their assignment), and 26 weeks is too short to prove long-term reduction in diabetes complications.

No severe low blood sugar occurred in either group, but the trial excluded people with a history of severe hypoglycemia or hypoglycemia unawareness (general medical guidance).
