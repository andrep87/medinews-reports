Blood Tests Revolutionize Early Alzheimer’s Diagnosis

"New blood tests measuring phosphorylated tau (p-tau217) [a protein fragment linked to Alzheimer's brain changes] can now help detect Alzheimer's pathology in adults with memory concerns, potentially replacing expensive PET scans [brain imaging] or invasive spinal taps as the first diagnostic step. A second biomarker (MTBR-tau243) is in development to help stage the disease like cancer. For patients, this means a simpler, less invasive path to diagnosis — but these tests are not yet validated for screening healthy people."

The FDA cleared two blood tests in 2026: Roche's Elecsys pTau217 and C2N's PrecivityAD2. PrecivityAD2 combines amyloid-beta 42/40 ratio and p-tau217 into an algorithm [not a single biomarker] to determine amyloid status in adults 55 and older with cognitive symptoms.
Lund University secured a $19.4 million NIH grant (2026-2031) to develop a blood test for MTBR-tau243 [a biomarker that reflects tau tangle buildup at the symptomatic stage]. This test is in development — not yet available clinically.
Researchers hypothesize that combining p-tau217 (which rises early, before symptoms) with MTBR-tau243 (which rises at the symptomatic stage) could eventually allow staging of Alzheimer's like cancer — distinguishing who has early pathology from who is actively declining. This is a research goal, not yet a clinical reality.
A Cleveland Clinic implementation study is testing p-tau217 as a triage tool [a first-step test] in primary care to reduce unnecessary specialist referrals and speed diagnosis for 1,800 older adults with cognitive concerns.
Current diagnosis relies on expensive PET scans or invasive spinal taps, creating long waits — UK patients average 3.5 years from first symptoms to diagnosis — while early anti-amyloid treatments (Leqembi, Kisunla) work best when started early.
Blood tests are minimally invasive, cheaper, and can be done in primary care, potentially expanding access to rural and underserved areas where neurologists and PET scanners are scarce.
Staging with two biomarkers (p-tau217 + MTBR-tau243) would tell patients not just “do I have Alzheimer’s pathology?” but “how far has the disease progressed?” — critical for prognosis and treatment choices.
Tear fluid and voice analysis are emerging as even less invasive screening tools, though still experimental and not yet ready for clinical use.
Adults 55 and older experiencing memory or thinking changes who are being evaluated for possible Alzheimer's — the FDA-cleared tests are indicated for this group, not for screening healthy people.
Patients considering anti-amyloid therapy [drugs that clear amyloid plaques from the brain] who need confirmation of amyloid pathology before starting treatment — blood tests can help determine who needs a confirmatory PET scan or spinal fluid test.
Women in midlife: new research shows menopause-related blood protein changes (including p-tau231 and inflammatory markers) correlate with later cognitive decline, suggesting a future role for midlife biomarker profiling — though clinical recommendations don't yet exist for this group.
Primary care physicians: blood tests can serve as a triage tool, but a positive result requires specialist interpretation. These are not standalone diagnostic tests.
Ask your primary care doctor: 'Should I have a cognitive screening at my next wellness visit, and if it's abnormal, would a blood biomarker test be appropriate before referring me to a specialist?'
If referred for memory evaluation, ask: 'Is a blood test for p-tau217 or PrecivityAD2 available here? Could it reduce the need for a PET scan or spinal tap?'
Do NOT order direct-to-consumer Alzheimer's blood tests or use online AI tools for self-diagnosis — they are not validated for screening healthy people and may cause unnecessary alarm or false reassurance.
If you're a woman in perimenopause or menopause experiencing cognitive symptoms (brain fog, word-finding difficulty), discuss these with your doctor — new research links menopause biology to brain health, though clinical screening recommendations don't yet exist for this group (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • Hope: A simple blood draw could replace months of specialist waits, letting patients start treatment earlier when it matters most.
  • Reality: Current anti-amyloid drugs slow decline modestly — they don't stop or reverse disease. Brain swelling/bleeding side effects (ARIA) require repeated MRI monitoring.

The Reality

  • Cost and access: Anti-amyloid drugs and blood tests are not yet widely reimbursed by insurance. Rural clinics may lack the infusion and imaging infrastructure needed for treatment (general medical guidance).
  • Equity: Most biomarker studies have underrepresented Black, Hispanic, Indigenous, and low-income populations — test performance in these groups needs validation before broad rollout (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Roche Elecsys pTau217: FDA-cleared single-biomarker blood test for amyloid status in symptomatic adults 55+.

C2N PrecivityAD2: FDA-cleared blood test combining amyloid-beta ratio and p-tau217; available through specialized laboratories. C2N expanding global partnerships (UK announced on World Alzheimer's Day).

MTBR-tau243 (Lund University): 5-year NIH-funded development program (2026-2031) to create a staging blood test. Not yet available clinically.

AI tools for ARIA monitoring [brain swelling/bleeding side effects from anti-amyloid drugs]: Being developed to help radiologists detect these changes on MRI scans during treatment (general medical guidance).

Voice analysis and tear fluid biomarkers are in early research as potential non-invasive screening tools — not yet clinically validated.

Evidence Uncertainty

PrecivityAD2 validation: High accuracy in study populations, but performance may vary in diverse real-world settings, particularly in patients with kidney disease or other conditions that affect blood protein levels.

MTBR-tau243: Still in development (5-year grant). Correlation with disease staging is the hypothesis being tested — not yet proven in large clinical cohorts.

Single-center limitation: The Cleveland Clinic implementation study will provide important data, but results from one health system may not generalize to all clinical settings.

Menopause proteomics: Discovery study included a small cohort (n=80 women); cross-sectional design cannot prove causation; findings need replication across diverse populations.

What To Watch

Lund University MTBR-tau243 development progress — will this staging biomarker reach FDA clearance within 5 years?

Cleveland Clinic implementation results — does p-tau217 triage in primary care actually reduce time-to-diagnosis?

Whether combination biomarker algorithms (integrating multiple blood markers) improve diagnostic and prognostic accuracy.

Tear fluid, voice analysis, and other non-invasive screening technologies moving through early validation.
