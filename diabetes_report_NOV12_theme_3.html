Beyond Daily Management: A Weekly Injection Tested in Type 1 Diabetes, and 14-Year Data on Surgery for Type 2

"Two studies look past day-to-day control toward more definitive intervention. A Phase 2 trial tested acmopatide — a once-weekly injection combining GLP-1 and GIP hormone action — as an add-on to insulin in adults with type 1 diabetes and overweight or obesity, improving blood sugar, reducing weight and lowering insulin needs. Separately, a pooled analysis of four randomised trials followed patients for up to 14 years and found metabolic-bariatric surgery produced diabetes remission in 58% of surgical patients versus 3% with intensive medical therapy. Both are about changing the underlying situation rather than managing it daily."

A Phase 2 trial tested acmopatide, a once-weekly injection that combines GLP-1 and GIP hormone action, added to insulin in adults with type 1 diabetes who also had overweight or obesity. It improved blood sugar control, reduced body weight, and lowered insulin requirements.
An important correction to how the results read: in the comparison group, average HbA1c DECREASED slightly, it did not rise (independent fact-check: the draft reported a 0.25% rise in the placebo group; the source shows a 0.25% decrease, which makes the drug's relative advantage smaller than the draft implied).
Separately, a pooled analysis of four randomised US trials (262 participants, median 11 years of follow-up, up to 14 years) found metabolic-bariatric surgery led to type 2 diabetes remission in 58% of surgical patients compared with 3% of those receiving intensive medical and lifestyle therapy.
The surgical analysis also suggested that earlier surgery was associated with better outcomes.
People with type 1 diabetes who also carry excess weight face a difficult combination: they need insulin, but insulin itself can promote weight gain, and few add-on options exist for them (general medical guidance).
An incretin-based weekly injection is a familiar class in type 2 diabetes, but its use in type 1 is investigational — type 1 is an autoimmune condition where the body makes little or no insulin, so the rationale is different (general medical guidance).
For type 2 diabetes, durable remission is rare with medication alone, so 14-year follow-up showing remission in a majority of surgical patients is a long-horizon result that most diabetes studies cannot offer.
Both findings speak to the same question patients ask: is there anything that changes the disease, rather than just managing it day to day? (general medical guidance)

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Tirzepatide (Mounjaro/Zepbound) — dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist approved for Type 2 diabetes and obesity; off-label use in Type 1 is growing but lacks trial data.

Roux-en-Y Gastric Bypass (RYGB): Gold standard for remission (69%); also best for reflux; higher nutrient deficiency risk (lifelong vitamins essential).

Sleeve Gastrectomy (SG): 52% remission; simpler surgery, fewer deficiencies, but higher reflux risk; now most common procedure in U.S.

Evidence Uncertainty

The acmopatide trial is Phase 2 — early-stage, and designed to assess signal rather than confirm benefit. The comparison group's HbA1c fell slightly, which narrows the apparent difference.

Adding an incretin drug to insulin in type 1 diabetes raises questions about low blood sugar and, in principle, ketoacidosis risk that larger trials would need to address (general medical guidance).

The surgical analysis pooled four randomised trials with 262 participants in total — long follow-up, but a modest sample, and the trials were not originally designed to be combined.

Remission in these analyses is defined by blood-sugar thresholds off medication; it is not the same as cure, and relapse can occur (general medical guidance).

What To Watch

Whether acmopatide advances to larger trials in type 1 diabetes, and what the safety profile looks like at scale (general medical guidance).

Longer follow-up on whether earlier surgery genuinely improves outcomes compared with later surgery (general medical guidance).

Whether incretin-based therapies gain any formal role as add-on treatment in type 1 diabetes (general medical guidance).

Data on how durable surgical remission remains beyond 14 years (general medical guidance).
