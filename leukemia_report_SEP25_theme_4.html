Vitamin C Shows Survival Signal in Pre-Leukemia Conditions (CCUS/MDS) — First Randomized Trial Supports Cancer Interception

"The first double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of oral vitamin C (1,000 mg daily) in people with pre-leukemia blood disorders [conditions where abnormal blood cells develop that can progress to acute leukemia] (CCUS or lower-risk MDS) found no effect on the size of the abnormal cell population but an encouraging exploratory signal: significantly better survival at roughly 3 years of follow-up and fewer serious side effects. While not yet practice-changing, this opens a potential 'cancer interception' strategy [intervening before cancer fully develops] using a safe, low-cost supplement."

The EVITA trial randomized 109 adults with clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance (CCUS) or lower-risk myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) to 1,000 mg oral vitamin C or matching placebo daily for 1 year; >50% were vitamin C deficient at baseline, and supplementation normalized levels.
Primary endpoint (clone size measured by variant allele frequency) did not differ between arms—vitamin C did not significantly shrink the pre-leukemic clone.
Exploratory analysis: vitamin C arm had significantly better overall survival at ~3 years follow-up and fewer serious adverse events during treatment and follow-up.
Vitamin C altered inflammatory cytokines/chemokines in ways linked to better outcomes; chronic inflammation drives clonal evolution, suggesting a modifiable pathway.
CCUS and lower-risk MDS have no approved therapies to prevent progression to AML; standard care is "watch and wait" with regular blood counts—frustrating for patients facing leukemia risk without actionable options.
Vitamin C is an essential cofactor for TET2, an enzyme that regulates DNA methylation and suppresses malignant transformation; TET2 mutations are common in clonal hematopoiesis and myeloid malignancies.
Preclinical data show vitamin C restores TET2 function and blocks leukemia progression when started early—supporting "cancer interception" before overt malignancy.
The survival signal, while hypothesis-generating, justifies a larger Phase III trial; if confirmed, a pennies-a-day supplement could change the natural history of pre-leukemia states.
Adults diagnosed with CCUS (unexplained cytopenia + somatic mutation <10% VAF, no MDS-defining features) or lower-risk MDS (IPSS-R very low/low/intermediate) not currently on disease-modifying therapy.
Older adults (median age ~70s in trial) who are ineligible for stem cell transplant and have limited options beyond monitoring.
Patients with documented vitamin C deficiency (common in this population due to inflammation, poor intake, or increased demand).
People with CHIP [clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential — a common age-related finding where a small population of abnormal blood cells exists without causing symptoms] may be interested, though the EVITA trial did not specifically study CHIP patients. Evidence Badges
Phase II randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (EVITA, n=109) Published in CANCER (American Cancer Society journal) Led by Van Andel Institute–SU2C Epigenetics Dream Team 4 Independent Sources

The Hope

  • Hope: A safe, inexpensive, accessible supplement could become the first intervention to improve survival in pre-leukemia states—transforming "watch and wait" into "monitor and modify."
  • Reality: Phase II exploratory finding ≠ proven benefit; Phase III will take years; many Phase II signals don't replicate. Vitamin C does not shrink the clone, so it may delay but not prevent leukemia.

The Reality

  • Safety: 1,000 mg daily is generally well tolerated. Sources note that the trial excluded patients with certain conditions; discuss suitability with your hematologist before starting (general medical guidance).
  • Access: OTC availability is a double-edged sword—easy to obtain but no quality standardization; patients may take it without medical supervision, missing monitoring for progression.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid): Oral 1,000 mg daily (standard over-the-counter dose); the EVITA trial used oral supplementation — not intravenous vitamin C — and achieved adequate blood levels.

Current standard for CCUS [clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance] and lower-risk MDS [myelodysplastic syndrome, an early-stage blood cancer]: Monitoring with regular blood counts; treatment when the condition progresses or when blood counts become dangerously low (general medical guidance).

The trial was led by the Van Andel Institute-Stand Up To Cancer Epigenetics Dream Team and published in CANCER (American Cancer Society journal).

A larger Phase III confirmatory trial is planned to determine whether the survival signal is real and to investigate the mechanism of benefit.

Evidence Uncertainty

Strengths: randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multi-center (Denmark, US); objective plasma vitamin C measures; pre-specified primary endpoint; long-term follow-up.

Limitations: n=109 (small), survival was exploratory not primary endpoint, not powered for OS; clone size (VAF) unchanged—mechanism of survival benefit unknown (inflammation? immune modulation? non-clonal effects?).

Generalizability: trial excluded active cancer treatment, severe renal impairment, history of kidney stones; results may not apply to higher-risk MDS or active AML.

No data on optimal dose, duration, or whether benefit requires baseline deficiency; 1,000 mg chosen based on pilot pharmacokinetic data.

What To Watch

Phase III EVITA confirmatory trial launch — this will be larger and designed to definitively answer whether vitamin C improves survival in pre-leukemia conditions.

Research into which patients benefit most — by mutation type, inflammation levels, or baseline vitamin C status.

Studies combining vitamin C with other treatments for higher-risk blood cancers (general medical guidance).

Whether regulatory agencies would consider a prevention indication for a dietary supplement if Phase III confirms the benefit.
