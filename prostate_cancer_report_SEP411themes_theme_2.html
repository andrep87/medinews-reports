Treatment Holidays After Deep Response to Hormone Therapy Are Feasible and Preserve Quality of Life

"For men with metastatic prostate cancer who achieve a deep, sustained response to modern hormone therapy (ADT [androgen deprivation therapy] plus an ARPI [androgen receptor pathway inhibitor]), a planned treatment holiday appears safe and effective in a small retrospective study. Median treatment-free survival was 35 months — longer than the 26 months patients spent on treatment — and one-third remained off therapy at five years. Restarting the same drugs after progression worked in 96% of cases, without triggering worse resistance. These findings are hypothesis-generating and need prospective trial validation."

Researchers analyzed 69 men with metastatic hormone-sensitive (mHSPC) or castration-resistant (mCRPC) prostate cancer who had received ADT [hormone therapy that lowers testosterone] plus an ARPI [a drug that blocks the androgen receptor, such as enzalutamide, abiraterone, or darolutamide] for at least 12 months with no progression and a deep response (very low PSA plus PSMA PET/CT [a specialized scan] showing no visible disease).
After stopping treatment, median treatment-free survival was 35 months; 56% were treatment-free at 2 years, 34% at 5 years. Note: this 35-month figure may reflect a specific subgroup (patients with prior prostate radiotherapy) rather than the overall cohort — the published breakdown should be consulted for details.
Of 33 men who progressed, 27 restarted the same ADT + ARPI combination; 96% responded again.
Men who were initially hormone-sensitive (mHSPC) had longer holidays; castration-resistant (mCRPC) patients had more than double the progression risk after stopping.
No evidence that stopping and restarting triggered worse drug resistance — disease control was re-established in nearly all who were rechallenged.
ADT + ARPI causes cumulative side effects: fatigue, hot flashes, muscle loss, bone thinning, sexual dysfunction, metabolic changes, and cardiovascular risk. A holiday of 2–5 years dramatically reduces this burden.
Financial burden also drops: oral ARPIs can cost thousands of dollars per month, and ADT injections require clinic visits. Stopping saves money and time (general medical guidance).
The 'deep responder' profile (very low PSA plus no visible disease on PSMA PET/CT) identifies who may safely pause — this is a biomarker-driven de-escalation strategy.
The fact that restarting the same drugs worked in 96% means the holiday is a true pause, not a dead end — the same effective regimen remains available.
Men with metastatic prostate cancer (hormone-sensitive or castration-resistant) who have been on ADT + ARPI for at least 12 months without progression.
Men with a deep response: PSA undetectable or very low, and PSMA PET/CT showing no visible disease (complete metabolic response).
Particularly applicable to men who were initially hormone-sensitive (mHSPC) and achieved a deep response.
Not for men with incomplete responses, high-volume disease in organs (visceral metastases), or rapidly rising PSA on therapy (general medical guidance).
If you have been on ADT + ARPI for over a year with excellent control (PSA <0.2 ng/mL, clear PSMA PET/CT), ask your oncologist: 'Am I a candidate for a planned treatment holiday? What are the monitoring rules?'
Request a PSMA PET/CT scan to document depth of response before discussing a holiday — this imaging is now widely available and often covered by insurance (general medical guidance).
Agree on a specific restart trigger with your doctor: PSA rise above a set threshold (such as >0.2 or >2 ng/mL), new lesions on imaging, or symptomatic progression (general medical guidance).
Clarify with your doctor exactly what stops during the holiday — this study stopped both ADT and ARPI, but some protocols may continue ADT injections. The approach varies (general medical guidance).
Track symptoms monthly (fatigue, bone pain, urinary changes) and keep scheduled PSA checks every 3 months. Do not skip monitoring.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

ARPIs used in deep-response cohorts: enzalutamide (Xtandi), abiraterone (Zytiga), darolutamide (Nubeqa), apalutamide (Erleada).

Newer approvals moving treatment earlier: Nubeqa + ADT now approved for mHSPC (ARANOTE, FDA approved June 3, 2025); Pluvicto (Lu-177 PSMA [a targeted radiation treatment]) approved for mHSPC (PSMAddition, FDA approved July 31, 2026) and pre-chemo mCRPC (PSMAfore, March 2025).

PARP inhibitor combinations [drugs that block cancer's ability to repair its DNA] (niraparib + abiraterone, talazoparib + enzalutamide, olaparib + abiraterone) approved for BRCA/HRR-mutated mCRPC.

The A-DREAM trial (ASCO 2026) offers more rigorous prospective data on treatment suspension: 78 patients, 57.7% treatment-free at 18 months, 38.5% still off treatment at a median 26.9 months.

Evidence Uncertainty

Strength: Consistent finding across cohort — median treatment-free survival (35 months) exceeded treatment duration (26 months); high rechallenge response (96%).

Limitation: Retrospective, single-institution, small sample (n=69); no randomization; selection bias toward fit, motivated patients. The 35-month TFS figure may come from a subgroup analysis (patients with prior prostate radiotherapy) rather than the overall cohort median.

Limitation: Heterogeneous ARPI types, treatment durations, and definitions of 'deep response' (PSMA PET criteria not uniform).

Unknown: Optimal monitoring frequency during holiday; whether intermittent ADT (vs. continuous) is safe; long-term survival impact vs. continuous therapy.

No prospective randomized trial data yet — this remains hypothesis-generating, not practice-changing on its own.

What To Watch

Prospective trials testing treatment holidays in deep responders (such as STAMPEDE protocol adaptations and cooperative group studies).

Biomarker refinement: ctDNA [circulating tumor DNA] clearance, PSMA PET complete response criteria, and genomic signatures predicting durable control off therapy.

ARASTEP trial (NCT05794906): testing Nubeqa + ADT in high-risk biochemical recurrence (PSMA PET-positive, conventional imaging-negative) — pushing effective therapy even earlier.

Long-term outcomes: Does intermittent therapy delay castration resistance? Does it extend overall survival vs. continuous therapy? (general medical guidance)
