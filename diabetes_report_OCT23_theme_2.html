First Weekly Basal Insulins Approved for Type 2 Diabetes

"The FDA has approved the first once-weekly basal insulins—insulin icodec (Awiqli®, Novo Nordisk) and insulin efsitora alfa (Onswik™, Eli Lilly)—for adults with type 2 diabetes. These ultra-long-acting insulins reduce injection burden from 365 to 52 per year while providing comparable glucose control and lower hypoglycemia risk versus daily basal insulins."

In March 2026, the FDA approved insulin icodec (Awiqli) as the first once-weekly basal insulin [a long-acting background insulin] for type 2 diabetes, based on the ONWARDS trial program (independent fact-check: verified against FDA/manufacturer sources, which the cited articles did not fully cover).
In September 2026, the FDA approved insulin efsitora alfa (Onswik) as the second once-weekly basal insulin, based on the QWINT trial program, which showed A1C control comparable to daily insulin degludec or glargine.
Both insulins are engineered to last a full week: icodec binds reversibly to the blood protein albumin to form a slow-release depot, while efsitora is fused to a protein fragment (Fc) that slows its clearance.
In their trials, weekly insulins achieved A1C reductions similar to daily insulin, with hypoglycemia rates broadly comparable to the daily comparators (independent fact-check: the draft's specific hypoglycemia rate ratios were not supported by the cited sources and have been generalized).
Daily basal insulin means about 365 injections a year; weekly insulin cuts that to about 52, easing a major barrier to starting and staying on insulin.
The long action provides a "forgiveness factor": if a dose is a day or two late, residual insulin activity remains, which may reduce the risk of a big blood-sugar spike compared with missing a daily dose (general medical guidance).
For people already on a weekly GLP-1 injection, a weekly basal insulin can be scheduled on the same day each week (general medical guidance).
Weekly dosing can simplify caregiver-assisted injections for older adults or those with dexterity, vision, or memory challenges (general medical guidance).
Adults with type 2 diabetes who are candidates for starting basal insulin after other therapies have not reached goal (general medical guidance).
People new to insulin who already use a weekly GLP-1 injection and prefer a similar schedule (general medical guidance).
People who have struggled with missed or mistimed daily basal insulin doses (general medical guidance).
Older adults or those with caregiver-assisted injections (general medical guidance).
Likely less suitable for people with rapidly changing insulin needs (for example, during acute illness or steroid use), because weekly insulin takes several weeks to reach steady state (general medical guidance).
Note: these weekly insulins are approved for type 2 diabetes and are not indicated for type 1 diabetes, where they carry a higher risk of severe low blood sugar.
If daily basal injections feel burdensome, you may wish to ask your clinician: "Could I switch to a weekly insulin like Awiqli or Onswik, and what would my starting dose be?" (general medical guidance).
If starting insulin for the first time, you may wish to ask your clinician to compare daily versus weekly options, including injection frequency, cost and coverage, and glucose monitoring (general medical guidance).
It helps to understand that these are concentrated insulins, so following the pen instructions exactly is important to avoid dosing errors (general medical guidance).
Guidelines recommend glucose monitoring (such as a CGM) for people on insulin; weekly insulin's long action makes glucose visibility especially useful (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • Weekly insulin is a meaningful convenience advance but does not remove the need for glucose monitoring, mealtime dosing (if used), or lifestyle management (general medical guidance).
  • Insurance coverage varies: some plans may place weekly insulins on higher cost tiers than daily insulins, so it helps to verify coverage before switching (general medical guidance).

The Reality

  • Because the insulin is concentrated, a dosing error could cause prolonged low blood sugar, so careful patient education and glucose monitoring are important safeguards (general medical guidance).
  • It is not a "set it and forget it" therapy: doses still need periodic adjustment, and illness, weight change, or new medications warrant clinician contact (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Insulin icodec (Awiqli, Novo Nordisk): a concentrated prefilled pen; reaches steady state over a few weeks; became available in 2026 (general medical guidance).

Insulin efsitora alfa (Onswik, Eli Lilly): available in two concentrations; similar weekly kinetics; U.S. availability following its September 2026 approval (general medical guidance).

Both are basal (background) insulins only; mealtime (bolus) insulin, if needed, remains a separate rapid-acting insulin before meals (general medical guidance).

Additional studies are examining long-term adherence, hypoglycemia, and quality of life with weekly insulins (general medical guidance).

Evidence Uncertainty

The ONWARDS (icodec) and QWINT (efsitora) Phase III programs each enrolled thousands of adults with type 2 diabetes and showed A1C reductions non-inferior to daily insulin (independent fact-check: the draft's specific per-trial sample sizes and hazard ratios were not supported by the cited sources).

Across the programs, hypoglycemia profiles were broadly similar to the daily comparators, with some trials suggesting favorable low-glucose rates for the weekly option (general medical guidance).

Uncertainties include long-term safety beyond 1-2 years, the best way to transition from daily to weekly dosing, and cost/coverage relative to daily insulins (general medical guidance).

These weekly insulins have not been established for type 1 diabetes or pregnancy, and dosing in significant kidney or liver impairment should follow the product labeling (general medical guidance).

What To Watch
