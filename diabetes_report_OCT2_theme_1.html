Continuous Glucose Monitoring Linked to Lower Death Risk in Type 2 Diabetes on Basal Insulin

"Real-world data from nearly 28,000 adults found that starting continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) [a wearable sensor that tracks glucose continuously] while on basal insulin [long-acting insulin taken once daily] was associated with a 44% lower risk of dying from any cause at one year and 35% lower at two years. CGM use was also associated with fewer heart attacks, strokes, and heart failure hospitalizations. This was an observational study, so it shows association rather than proven cause."

Researchers analyzed U.S. electronic health records of 13,935 adults with type 2 diabetes who started CGM and matched them one-to-one with 13,935 similar patients who did not use CGM.
At 12 months, 1.16% of CGM users had died versus 2.09% of non-users — a 44% lower relative risk. At 24 months the gap narrowed somewhat, to 35% lower (2.12% vs 3.26%).
First heart attacks, strokes, or other major cardiovascular events were 26% lower at 12 months and 24% lower at 24 months among CGM users.
Heart failure hospitalizations showed the largest difference: 54% lower at 12 months and 47% lower at 24 months.
This is among the first evidence connecting CGM — already known to improve day-to-day glucose control — to survival and hard cardiovascular outcomes in type 2 diabetes on basal insulin.
People on basal insulin often have highs and lows that fingerstick checks miss entirely. CGM provides continuous feedback, allowing adjustments before problems escalate (general medical guidance).
The association appeared across all measured complications, suggesting the relationship extends beyond glucose numbers alone — though an observational study cannot establish why.
Adults with type 2 diabetes currently using basal (long-acting) insulin, with or without other non-insulin diabetes medications.
People who have difficulty keeping glucose in target range or experience frequent highs or lows (general medical guidance).
The matched groups had an average age of 58 and were 47% female, matched on 29 clinical factors.
Ask your clinician: 'Am I a candidate for a CGM sensor to use with my basal insulin?'
If prescribed, wear the sensor consistently before your next visit and bring the glucose trend report to discuss dose adjustments (general medical guidance).
Use CGM alerts to catch low glucose early and treat it before it becomes severe — this is one of the main practical advantages over fingerstick testing (general medical guidance).
Check whether your insurance covers CGM for type 2 diabetes on basal insulin, and ask about patient assistance programs if cost is a barrier (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • Hope: CGM is a non-drug tool associated with meaningfully better survival and fewer heart failure hospitalizations, with differences appearing within the first year.
  • Reality: Association is not causation. A randomized trial would be needed to establish that CGM itself drives the benefit rather than the characteristics of people who use it.

The Reality

  • Reality: Access barriers persist — cost, prior authorizations, and gaps in clinician awareness mean many eligible patients still do not get CGM (general medical guidance).
  • Reality: CGM requires engagement. Wearing the sensor and reviewing the data is part of the work, and it does not replace medication adherence or healthy habits (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

CGM sensors provide glucose readings continuously via a smartphone or handheld reader, replacing or reducing the need for fingerstick checks (general medical guidance).

This analysis used the Truveta health data platform in collaboration with Abbott, applying a target trial emulation design — a statistical approach that attempts to mimic a randomized trial using real-world records.

The findings were presented at the EASD 2026 annual meeting and have not yet been published in a peer-reviewed journal.

Evidence Uncertainty

Strengths: Nearly 28,000 patients, matched on 29 variables, with results consistent across sensitivity analyses including exclusion of the COVID period and early deaths.

Critical limitation: This is an observational study and cannot prove CGM causes the survival benefit. People who start CGM may differ systematically from those who do not — in engagement with their care, health literacy, or access to resources — in ways matching cannot fully capture.

Unmeasured factors including diet, medication adherence, and socioeconomic status may contribute. Medication changes during follow-up were not tracked.

Results may not generalize to people without insurance access or to non-U.S. health systems.

Presented at a conference rather than published, so the full methodology has not undergone peer review.

What To Watch

Prospective randomized trials that could confirm whether the mortality association reflects a true causal benefit.

Peer-reviewed publication of this analysis with full methodological detail.

Expansion of insurance coverage for CGM in type 2 diabetes beyond intensive insulin regimens (general medical guidance).

Longer-term outcomes from ongoing real-world CGM studies.
