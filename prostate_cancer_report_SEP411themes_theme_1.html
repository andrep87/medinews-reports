Active Surveillance Becomes the Standard for Low-Risk Prostate Cancer, But Many Men Still Miss Out

"A landmark VA study shows 93% of veterans with low-risk prostate cancer now choose active surveillance — monitoring with regular tests instead of immediate surgery or radiation — up from just 27% two decades ago. This approach delivers the same survival while avoiding incontinence, erectile dysfunction, and bowel problems. However, outside the VA system, fewer than 60% of eligible U.S. men receive this option, and Black and Hispanic patients are less likely to be offered it regardless of cancer features."

Researchers tracked over 73,000 veterans diagnosed with low-risk or favorable intermediate-risk prostate cancer across the VA system from 2005 to 2024.
In 2024, 93% of veterans with low-risk disease chose active surveillance — regular PSA [prostate-specific antigen] blood tests, MRI scans, and repeat biopsies [tissue samples] — instead of immediate treatment, up from 27% in 2005.
For favorable intermediate-risk disease, active surveillance use rose from 14% to 61% over the same period.
The VA's integrated system — shared records, quality tracking, and long-term doctor-patient continuity — drove adoption far beyond the national average, where under 60% of low-risk men receive surveillance.
Low-risk prostate cancer (Gleason Grade Group 1 [a measure of how aggressive cancer cells look under a microscope]) almost never spreads. Treating it immediately exposes men to lifelong side effects without a survival benefit.
Active surveillance avoids urinary incontinence [leaking urine], erectile dysfunction [trouble getting or keeping an erection], and bowel issues from surgery or radiation in men whose cancer would never threaten their life.
The VA data strongly suggest that a system-wide commitment to guideline-based care can nearly eliminate overtreatment — but most U.S. men are treated in fragmented systems where surveillance rates lag.
Men diagnosed with low-risk prostate cancer: PSA under 10 ng/mL, Gleason Grade Group 1 (3+3=6), and cancer confined to a small portion of the prostate.
Men with favorable intermediate-risk disease: Grade Group 2 (3+4=7) in a limited number of biopsy cores, PSA under 10 ng/mL — surveillance rose to 55–88% depending on specific features.
Black and Hispanic men, and those in economically disadvantaged areas, were less likely to be offered surveillance even with identical cancer features — this disparity needs active attention.
If your PSA is elevated, ask your doctor: 'Should I get an MRI and biomarker tests (like the 4Kscore, PHI, or new PSB blood test) before deciding on a biopsy?'
If diagnosed with Grade Group 1 cancer, ask: 'Am I a candidate for active surveillance? What is the monitoring schedule — PSA every 3–6 months, MRI at 12 months, repeat biopsy at 12–18 months?'
Request a clear explanation of your Gleason Grade Group and whether your cancer meets criteria for surveillance (Grade Group 1, or favorable Grade Group 2 with low volume).
If you are Black, have a family history, or carry a BRCA gene mutation, start screening discussions at age 40–45 per guidelines — do not wait until 50.

The Hope

  • Hope: Most men with low-risk disease can safely avoid treatment side effects for decades; many never need intervention.
  • Reality: Surveillance requires discipline — missed appointments or biopsies risk missing progression. Anxiety about 'untreated cancer' is real and leads some to request treatment anyway.

The Reality

  • Reality: Outside VA systems, you may need to advocate for surveillance; some urology practices still treat all low-risk patients immediately.
  • Reality: Disparities persist — Black men are about 70% more likely to be diagnosed and twice as likely to die from prostate cancer, partly due to later-stage diagnosis and unequal access to guideline-concordant care.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Active surveillance protocol: PSA every 3–6 months, digital rectal exam annually, prostate MRI at 12–18 months, systematic plus targeted biopsy at 12–18 months then every 2–5 years (general medical guidance).

New blood test (5Q Spectra Prostate Dx/PSB): a lab-developed test available now; AUC 0.86 alone, 0.92 combined with AI and imaging; may eliminate up to 44% of unnecessary biopsies after an elevated PSA.

PSMA PET/CT [a specialized scan that detects prostate cancer spread] (Pylarify, Illuccix): now standard for staging; detects spread earlier than CT or bone scan, especially when PSA is low.

Triggers for treatment on surveillance: grade upgrade to Group 2 or higher, significant volume increase on MRI/biopsy, or PSA doubling time under 3 years (general medical guidance).

Evidence Uncertainty

VA study: 73,000+ patients, 19-year span, real-world data showing survival equivalence to treatment. Supported by the PIVOT trial (surgery vs. observation, 20-year follow-up, no mortality difference).

Limitation: VA population is older, predominantly male, and system-integrated — results may not generalize to younger men or fee-for-service settings.

Surveillance disparities: Black/Hispanic veterans and those in high-disadvantage areas had lower surveillance odds independent of clinical factors — cause unclear (access, communication, bias?).

The long-term psychological burden of 'living with cancer' is unmeasured; some men still choose treatment due to anxiety despite counseling.

What To Watch

Validation of the 5Q Spectra PSB blood test and other liquid biopsies (urine, blood) to further reduce unnecessary biopsies.

Trials testing whether genomic classifiers (Decipher, Oncotype DX GPS) can better predict which Grade Group 1 tumors harbor hidden aggressive features.

National efforts to close the surveillance gap: quality metrics, payment reform, and shared decision-making tools for community practices.

Long-term follow-up of the VA cohort: 20+ year metastasis and mortality data by surveillance vs. treatment choice.
