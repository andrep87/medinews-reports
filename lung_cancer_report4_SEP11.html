FDA Approves Sevabertinib as First-Line Treatment for HER2-Mutant Non-Squamous NSCLC

"The FDA has expanded approval of sevabertinib (Hyrnuo) to include previously untreated patients with advanced non-squamous NSCLC [a type of non-small cell lung cancer] whose tumors carry HER2 tyrosine kinase domain mutations. This oral targeted therapy now offers a first-line option for a molecular subset historically linked to poor outcomes and brain metastases, with a 75% response rate and durable responses in the SOHO-01 trial."

On September 9, 2026, the FDA gave accelerated approval to sevabertinib (Hyrnuo) for adults with advanced non-squamous NSCLC whose tumors carry HER2 (ERBB2) tyrosine kinase domain activating mutations [specific genetic changes that drive cancer growth]. This expands its earlier approval (November 2025) for previously treated patients to include those who have not yet received any treatment.
Approval was based on the open-label, single-arm SOHO-01 trial. Among 69 previously untreated patients who took sevabertinib 20 mg twice daily, 75% saw their tumors shrink (95% CI 64–85%). Of those who responded, 73% maintained their response for at least 6 months and 38% for at least 12 months.
The FDA reviewed the application under Project Orbis alongside the UK medicines regulator (MHRA), and granted Priority Review, Breakthrough Therapy, and Orphan Drug designations.
Sevabertinib is the second HER2-targeted pill approved for first-line use in this setting, following zongertinib (Hernexeos, Boehringer Ingelheim), which received accelerated approval in February 2026.
HER2 mutations occur in roughly 2–6% of NSCLC cases and are linked to aggressive disease, including higher rates of brain metastases and historically poor outcomes with standard chemotherapy.
Until now, patients with HER2-mutant NSCLC typically received platinum-based chemotherapy first, with targeted therapy reserved for later use. This approval moves a highly active, brain-penetrant oral drug to the front line where it can do the most good.
The 75% response rate is roughly double the typical chemotherapy response rate (about 30–40%) and offers a convenient oral alternative to intravenous antibody-drug conjugates like trastuzumab deruxtecan (Enhertu), which requires infusion center visits and carries interstitial lung disease risk (general medical guidance).
Comprehensive molecular testing at diagnosis (tissue biopsy or blood-based liquid biopsy) is now essential to identify eligible patients early, as these specific HER2 mutations require specialized genomic testing for detection.
Adults with advanced (unresectable stage III or stage IV) non-squamous NSCLC whose tumors test positive for HER2 (ERBB2) tyrosine kinase domain activating mutations via an FDA-authorized test.
Patients who have not received prior systemic therapy for advanced disease. Those who completed adjuvant or neoadjuvant chemotherapy more than 6 months prior may be eligible based on the trial criteria.
The approval covers non-squamous histology (mostly adenocarcinoma); squamous cell lung cancers were excluded from the pivotal trial.
HER2 mutations are more common in never/light smokers, younger patients, and Asian populations, but testing is recommended for all non-squamous NSCLC regardless of clinical features (general medical guidance).
If newly diagnosed with advanced non-squamous NSCLC, ask your oncology team: 'Has my tumor been tested for HER2 tyrosine kinase domain mutations using a comprehensive genomic panel or FDA-approved liquid biopsy?' Request testing if not yet done.
If you have a known HER2 mutation and have not started treatment, discuss with your doctor whether sevabertinib is appropriate as first-line therapy, and whether clinical trial options should also be considered.
Ask about a brain MRI at baseline if not already done, since HER2-mutant NSCLC has a high tendency to spread to the brain and sevabertinib has shown activity against brain metastases.
Ask about patient assistance programs through Bayer for help with out-of-pocket costs, as oral targeted therapies can be expensive even with insurance. Confirm whether coverage is through your pharmacy benefit or medical benefit.

Research Progress

Sevabertinib (Hyrnuo, Bayer): Oral HER2-selective targeted drug, approved under accelerated approval for both first-line and previously treated HER2 mutation-positive NSCLC. Strengths: 75% tumor shrinkage rate (95% CI 64–85%) in 69 treatment-naive patients; 73% of responders maintained response for at least 6 months; intracranial responses observed. The large effect size supports accelerated approval despite the single-arm design.

Limitations: Single-arm trial without a comparison group; overall survival data not yet mature; median follow-up not reported; no randomized progression-free or overall survival data yet. The confirmatory SOHO-02 trial is required for full approval.

Safety: Grade 3 or higher treatment-related side effects in about 30% of patients. Notable risks include diarrhea, liver toxicity, interstitial lung disease/pneumonitis (about 3–5%), heart function changes, eye problems, pancreatic enzyme elevation, and harm to a developing fetus.

Unknowns: Best treatment sequence after sevabertinib stops working; how well it works against specific HER2 mutation subtypes (for example, exon 20 insertions vs. point mutations); potential combination strategies with chemotherapy or immunotherapy.

Tarlatamab Plus Durvalumab Shows Survival Benefit in First-Line Extensive-Stage SCLC

"The Phase 3 DeLLphi-305 trial met its primary endpoint: adding tarlatamab (Imdelltra), a DLL3-targeted bispecific T-cell engager [a drug that physically bridges immune cells to cancer cells], to durvalumab (Imfinzi) maintenance significantly improved overall survival in extensive-stage small cell lung cancer after initial chemo-immunotherapy. This is the first Phase 3 survival win for a BiTE therapy in frontline SCLC. Full data (including the size of the survival benefit) have not yet been publicly released."

In the global, randomized, open-label DeLLphi-305 trial, 563 patients with extensive-stage SCLC [the most advanced form of small cell lung cancer] who did not progress after 4 cycles of induction therapy (durvalumab + platinum chemotherapy + etoposide) were randomized 1:1 to maintenance durvalumab alone vs. durvalumab plus tarlatamab.
At a pre-specified interim analysis, the combination met the primary endpoint of statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvement in overall survival. Progression-free survival and objective response rate were also positive. No specific hazard ratios, median survival figures, or p-values have been publicly released yet — only the topline result that the primary endpoint was met.
Safety was consistent with the known profiles of each drug; no new safety signals emerged. Post-infusion monitoring time for tarlatamab was reduced to 1–2 hours (compared to 22–24 hours required in the current US label for second-line use).
Amgen and AstraZeneca plan to present full data at an upcoming medical congress and submit to regulatory authorities for first-line maintenance approval.
ES-SCLC is aggressive: median survival is about 12 months with current standard treatment (durvalumab + chemotherapy followed by durvalumab maintenance). Only about 40% of patients survive long enough to receive second-line therapy (general medical guidance).
Moving tarlatamab (currently approved for second-line and beyond) into first-line maintenance attacks the disease earlier, when tumor burden is lower and the immune system may be more capable of responding.
The combination pairs two complementary immune approaches: durvalumab (a PD-L1 checkpoint inhibitor [releases the brakes on immune cells]) while tarlatamab (a DLL3×CD3 BiTE [physically drags immune T cells to DLL3-expressing cancer cells]). DLL3 is found on the surface of SCLC cells in about 85–96% of patients.
If approved, this would be the first new frontline overall survival improvement in ES-SCLC since CASPIAN (durvalumab + chemotherapy) in 2020, and the first BiTE therapy to demonstrate a survival benefit in any solid tumor first-line setting.
Adults with newly diagnosed extensive-stage SCLC (stage IV, or stage III too extensive for curative chemoradiation) who have completed 4 cycles of platinum/etoposide + durvalumab induction without disease progression.
Patients with treated or untreated asymptomatic brain metastases were eligible in the trial — important, as SCLC frequently spreads to the brain.
Adequate organ function and performance status (ECOG 0–1) required.
DLL3 expression testing is not required for enrollment (DLL3 is expressed in the vast majority of SCLC), though future biomarker studies may identify patients who benefit most.
If newly diagnosed with ES-SCLC, confirm your treatment plan includes 4 cycles of platinum (carboplatin or cisplatin) + etoposide + durvalumab followed by durvalumab maintenance — this is the current standard of care.
Ask your oncologist: 'Am I a candidate for the DeLLphi-305 regimen (adding tarlatamab to durvalumab maintenance) once it receives FDA approval, or can I access it via clinical trial now?'
Request brain MRI at baseline and every 2–3 months during maintenance, as SCLC has a high risk of spreading to the brain. Early detection allows targeted radiation while continuing systemic therapy (general medical guidance).
Discuss cytokine release syndrome (CRS) [an immune overreaction that can cause fever, low blood pressure, and breathing difficulty] and neurotoxicity monitoring: tarlatamab requires step-up dosing (starting low and increasing over the first cycle) with 1–2 hour observation after early infusions.

Osimertinib Plus Chemotherapy Doubles Progression-Free Survival in EGFR/TP53-Mutant NSCLC

"The Phase 3 TOP trial showed that adding platinum-pemetrexed chemotherapy to first-line osimertinib (Tagrisso) more than doubles progression-free survival (34 vs 15.6 months) in advanced EGFR-mutated NSCLC with concurrent TP53 mutations — a subgroup historically resistant to EGFR targeted therapy alone. This supports risk-adapted intensification of treatment for molecularly defined high-risk patients."

In the Phase 3 TOP study presented at the European Lung Cancer Congress (ELCC) 2026, 294 treatment-naive patients with stage IV or recurrent EGFR-mutated (exon 19 deletion or L858R) non-squamous NSCLC who also carried TP53 mutations [a common co-occurring genetic change linked to more aggressive disease] were randomly assigned to receive either osimertinib 80 mg daily alone or osimertinib plus pemetrexed/carboplatin chemotherapy (4 cycles), followed by maintenance osimertinib plus pemetrexed.
Median progression-free survival [time until the cancer worsens] was 34.0 months with the combination vs. 15.6 months with osimertinib alone (HR 0.44; 95% CI 0.32–0.61; P<0.001). This benefit held across subgroups, including patients with brain metastases.
Tumor shrinkage rate was higher with the combination (82.9% vs. about 72%), and median duration of response doubled (32.7 vs. 15.3 months).
Overall survival data were not yet mature (30.6% of events reached), but an early trend favored the combination (HR 0.57; 95% CI 0.37–0.87).
Grade 3 or higher treatment-related side effects: 62.4% with the combination vs. 14.9% with osimertinib alone (primarily blood count drops from chemotherapy). No new safety signals.
TP53 co-mutations occur in roughly 40–50% of EGFR-mutant NSCLC and are linked to poorer outcomes with targeted therapy alone — shorter progression-free survival and a higher risk of the cancer transforming into small cell histology (general medical guidance).
The earlier FLAURA2 trial showed that adding chemotherapy to osimertinib benefits the overall EGFR-mutant population (PFS 25.5 vs 18.9 months). The TOP trial shows that the TP53-mutant subgroup derives an even greater benefit (PFS 34 vs 15.6 months), supporting biomarker-driven treatment intensification.
This moves the field toward 'risk-adapted' first-line therapy: patients with high-risk features (TP53 mutation, high tumor burden, brain metastases) get chemotherapy plus targeted therapy upfront, while lower-risk patients may avoid chemotherapy toxicity with targeted therapy alone (general medical guidance).
The combination also showed strong activity against brain metastases, which is important since TP53 mutations are associated with higher rates of brain spread.
Patients with newly diagnosed stage IV or recurrent non-squamous NSCLC with classic EGFR sensitizing mutations (exon 19 deletion or L858R) AND concurrent TP53 mutations found on comprehensive genomic profiling (tissue or liquid biopsy).
Patients must be fit for platinum-based chemotherapy (adequate kidney function, performance status 0–1, no prohibitive other illnesses).
Those with asymptomatic brain metastases at baseline were included and appeared to benefit; patients with symptomatic or untreated brain metastases typically need local treatment first (general medical guidance).
TP53 testing requires a comprehensive genomic panel (it is not detected by EGFR-only PCR tests). Make sure your molecular profiling includes TP53.
If newly diagnosed with EGFR-mutant NSCLC, ask: 'Was my tumor tested for TP53 mutations on a comprehensive genomic panel (not just EGFR)?' If not, request tissue re-biopsy or liquid biopsy for TP53 status.
If TP53-mutant, discuss with your oncologist whether upfront osimertinib plus carboplatin/pemetrexed (the TOP regimen) is appropriate vs. osimertinib alone.
Request a baseline brain MRI if not done. If brain metastases are found, discuss whether to start the combination therapy immediately or treat the brain first with focused radiation.
Prepare for chemotherapy side effects: ask about growth factor support (G-CSF injections to prevent dangerously low white blood cell counts), anti-nausea medications, and blood count monitoring. Consider fertility preservation if applicable.

Lung Cancer Screening Advances: Awareness Campaigns, Mathematical Modeling, and Equity Initiatives

"Multiple efforts are addressing the critical gap in lung cancer screening uptake: a bilingual community campaign ('Saved by the Scan') increased screenings 50% in two years; a new mathematical 'cure threshold' model aims to enable personalized screening intervals; a $3.7 million NIH grant targets racial disparities in screening access; and advocacy pushes for national programs globally."

'Saved by the Scan' campaign (Baptist Health/FIU): A bilingual, multi-channel outreach effort (email, social media, mail, billboards) linked to 31 imaging centers increased annual low-dose CT (LDCT) screenings from 2,185 (2021) to 3,285 (2023) — a 50% jump in a 68% Hispanic community where 1 in 5 residents is linguistically isolated.
Mathematical 'cure threshold' model (FIU/Deborah Goldwasser): Uses LDCT data to correct statistical biases in older X-ray-based models. It identifies the precise window when fast-growing tumors are still surgically curable, potentially enabling personalized screening intervals — more frequent for aggressive tumors, less frequent for slow-growing ones.
NIH $3.7 million R01 grant (Hackensack Meridian/CDI): 'Witness Project Lung' partners with 22 churches and community organizations in NJ/NYC to train cancer survivors and lay health advisors to boost screening in African American communities (where the current screening rate is reported as under 2%, compared to the roughly 5–18% national average).
The American Lung Association is expanding its 'Awareness, Trust and Action' campaign highlighting that only 1% of lung cancer trial participants are Hispanic, and that Black and Hispanic patients have worse early-diagnosis and survival rates.
Irish advocate Gillian Ryan highlights that lung cancer kills more people in Ireland than any other cancer (about 2,000/year), yet lacks a national screening program.
LDCT screening reduces lung cancer deaths by 20% (NLST trial) and 24% (NELSON trial), but more than 80% of eligible Americans never get screened — the largest implementation gap in cancer prevention.
Finding lung cancer early (while still confined to the lung) yields 60–65% five-year survival vs. under 10% for cancer that has already spread. Every month of delay reduces the chance of cure (general medical guidance).
Standard annual screening ignores tumor biology: fast-growing tumors may become incurable between annual scans. Goldwasser's 'cure threshold' model could eventually tailor intervals to individual risk.
Racial disparities are profound: Black Americans have higher lung cancer incidence and death rates but lower screening rates. Trust, access, and culturally sensitive approaches are needed to close this gap.
USPSTF-eligible adults: Age 50–80, with a 20 pack-year or greater smoking history (for example, 1 pack/day for 20 years or 2 packs/day for 10 years), who are current smokers or quit within the last 15 years.
High-priority underserved groups: African American communities (very low screening rates), Hispanic/Latino communities (language and cultural barriers), rural populations (distance to LDCT centers), and uninsured or underinsured individuals.
People with additional risk factors: radon exposure, workplace carcinogens (asbestos, silica), family history of lung cancer, prior chest radiation, COPD, or pulmonary fibrosis.
The mathematical model may eventually expand screening criteria beyond age and pack-years to include tumor growth rates, genetic risk scores, and environmental exposures (general medical guidance).
Check your eligibility: Use the American Lung Association's 'Saved by the Scan' online tool (savedbythescan.org) or ask your primary care doctor: 'Do I qualify for a low-dose CT lung cancer screening based on my smoking history?'
If eligible, schedule the scan: It takes about 5 minutes, requires no preparation, no IV, and uses low radiation (about 1.5 mSv, compared to 7 mSv for a standard CT). Most insurers cover it with $0 copay for eligible patients.
If you currently smoke, combine screening with quitting: Ask your doctor about FDA-approved medications (varenicline, bupropion, nicotine replacement therapy) plus counseling — quitting reduces future risk and improves the benefit of screening (general medical guidance).
Advocate for others: Share screening information with eligible friends and family. Support community programs. Contact legislators to fund national screening programs, especially if you live outside the US.

The Hope

  • Hope: Screening is the single most effective tool to reduce lung cancer deaths. Community campaigns work. Mathematical models can personalize prevention. Blood tests may someday simplify access.
  • Reality: Only an estimated 5–18% of eligible Americans are screened. Stigma ('smoker's disease') reduces advocacy and funding. False positives cause anxiety and sometimes invasive procedures. Rural areas lack LDCT centers. Medicaid coverage varies by state (general medical guidance).

The Reality

  • Equity: Black patients are often diagnosed younger but may be ineligible under current age and pack-year criteria. Hispanic patients face language and navigation barriers. Globally, most countries have no screening program.
  • Sustainability: Screening requires ongoing funding, workforce (radiologists, patient navigators), quality assurance, and integration with cessation and treatment pathways (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Screening technology: LDCT is the standard. Emerging blood-based tests (such as Guardant Shield, which detects methylation patterns in circulating tumor DNA) are being studied as a complement to LDCT, not a replacement.

AI-assisted nodule evaluation: Deep learning tools reduce false positives and radiologist workload. Several FDA-cleared AI tools are increasingly used in lung cancer screening programs.

Risk prediction models: Established models (PLCOm2012, Lung-RADS 2.0, Brock University model) focus on who to screen. Goldwasser's 'cure threshold' model adds a time dimension — when to screen.

Implementation science: Patient navigation, shared decision-making visits, electronic health record-based eligibility flags, and mobile LDCT units for rural areas are being tested and deployed.

Evidence Uncertainty

Strengths: NLST and NELSON are large randomized controlled trials providing the highest level of evidence for mortality reduction. 'Saved by the Scan' demonstrates a real-world 50% screening uptake increase with community engagement. The mathematical model is grounded in LDCT natural history data.

Limitations: The community campaign was at a single health system. The mathematical model has not yet been validated prospectively. Witness Project Lung is just starting (5-year grant). Screening harms include false positives (about 96% of positive LDCTs turn out to be benign), incidental findings, overdiagnosis (estimated at about 18% in NLST), and low-level radiation exposure.

Uncertainties: Optimal screening interval for different risk groups; cost-effectiveness of personalized vs. annual screening; long-term adherence to tailored schedules; blood test performance in high-risk vs. average-risk populations.

Blood-based screening tests: WCLC 2026 data on Guardant Shield and other assays in high-risk cohorts. Potential Medicare coverage decisions to follow.
