Cancer Incidence Gap Widens for People With Type 2 Diabetes, Driven by Obesity-Related Cancers

"A nationwide Danish study of 75,540 cancers in people with type 2 diabetes over 27 years shows cancer incidence is now 12% higher in men and 22% higher in women versus those without diabetes—a gap that has widened since 1996-2009. Obesity-related cancers (colorectal, postmenopausal breast, pancreatic, liver, endometrial) account for most of the excess risk."

Researchers analyzed nationwide Danish registry data (1996-2022) comparing 75,540 first cancers in people with type 2 diabetes versus 635,323 in those without diabetes.
Age-standardized cancer incidence [cancer rates adjusted for age differences between groups] was 12% higher in men and 22% higher in women with type 2 diabetes during 2010-2022 — a gap that has widened compared to 9% and 15% during 1996-2009.
Obesity-related cancers drove the gap: 45% higher in men and 30% higher in women with type 2 diabetes (2010-2022), up from 34% and 23% in the earlier period.
Pancreatic cancer showed the largest relative excess: 2.65 times higher in men and 2.58 times higher in women with type 2 diabetes. Colorectal cancer was 17%/14% higher; lung cancer 23%/25% higher.
Prostate cancer incidence was 19% lower in men with type 2 diabetes — the reasons are not fully understood.
Cancer has become a leading cause of death among people with type 2 diabetes as heart disease mortality has declined with better treatments.
The widening gap suggests cancer is an increasingly important long-term complication of type 2 diabetes, requiring systematic attention alongside heart and kidney disease.
Obesity-related cancers (colorectal, breast, pancreatic) represent a shared pathway where excess weight, insulin resistance [when the body doesn't respond well to insulin], and chronic inflammation may promote cancer growth (general medical guidance).
Existing cancer screening programs (colonoscopy, mammography) could reduce cancer deaths if participation increases among people with type 2 diabetes.
All adults with type 2 diabetes — the study shows elevated cancer incidence across the population, not just in specific subgroups.
Women with type 2 diabetes face a larger relative increase (22% vs. 12% in men).
The study does not establish type 2 diabetes as a direct cause of cancer — shared risk factors (obesity, smoking, inactivity) contribute — but identifies people with type 2 diabetes as a higher-risk group for targeted prevention.
Ask your primary care provider or diabetes specialist: 'Am I up to date on all recommended cancer screenings for my age and risk factors?'
Stay current with age-appropriate screening: the study highlights colorectal, breast, pancreatic, and lung cancers as having the largest excess risk in people with type 2 diabetes.
Address modifiable shared risk factors: weight management, physical activity, smoking cessation, alcohol moderation, and blood sugar control (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • Hope: Identifying type 2 diabetes as a marker for higher cancer risk could focus prevention efforts on a population that's already in regular medical contact.
  • Reality: Cancer screening participation is often lower in people with diabetes, who may already feel overwhelmed by medical visits and medication management (general medical guidance).

The Reality

  • Reality: The cancer-diabetes link is complex — improving blood sugar control alone may not reduce cancer risk if obesity and inflammation persist (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Danish registry study (presented at EASD 2026 [the European Association for the Study of Diabetes annual meeting]): 27-year follow-up showing widening cancer incidence gap in type 2 diabetes. Note: this is a conference presentation, not yet a peer-reviewed publication.

Obesity-related cancers account for the majority of excess risk — weight management interventions are being studied for cancer prevention in people with diabetes (general medical guidance).

Whether diabetes medications (such as metformin or GLP-1 receptor agonists) modify cancer risk is an active area of research, but no medication is approved or recommended specifically for cancer prevention in diabetes (general medical guidance).

Evidence Uncertainty

Strengths: Nationwide registry data covering 27 years; large sample (75,540 cancers in diabetes, 635,323 in non-diabetes); age-standardized comparisons.

Limitations: Observational data — cannot prove type 2 diabetes directly causes cancer. Shared risk factors (obesity, smoking, inactivity) likely contribute. The study was presented at EASD 2026 and may not yet be peer-reviewed.

The widening gap could partly reflect improved survival from cardiovascular disease (patients live longer, giving more time to develop cancer) or changes in screening patterns (general medical guidance).

Prostate cancer was lower in men with type 2 diabetes — the reasons are speculative and not explained in the source.

What To Watch

Full peer-reviewed publication of the Danish cancer incidence data and potential replication in other populations.

Whether diabetes guidelines incorporate cancer screening recommendations more explicitly.

Results from studies examining whether weight loss interventions (including newer diabetes medications) reduce cancer incidence in people with type 2 diabetes (general medical guidance).
