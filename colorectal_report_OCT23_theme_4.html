Liquid Biopsy Comes of Age: ctDNA and Metabolic Biomarkers Guide Treatment Decisions and Predict Recurrence

"Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) [tiny fragments of cancer DNA in the blood] and a new metabolic blood signature are moving toward clinical decision-making. ctDNA is being studied to guide adjuvant therapy, to detect molecular relapse months before scans, and to select patients for targeted retreatment. A separate study found that a network of circulating amino acids from a single pre-surgery blood sample may help predict recurrence risk. Most of these uses are still being validated in trials."

An international molecular tumor board (October 2026) brought together experts to discuss real-world use of ctDNA for monitoring, treatment selection, and detecting residual disease (general medical guidance).
MD Anderson's new colorectal medical oncology department highlighted ctDNA as part of its disease-specific strategy (general medical guidance).
The PARERE study (GONO) is comparing ctDNA-guided panitumumab retreatment vs regorafenib sequencing in chemorefractory mCRC — using ctDNA to decide which later-line therapy.
BRICKET trial (ESMO 2026): ctDNA-guided retreatment with encorafenib + cetuximab in BRAF V600E mCRC — only patients with BRAF V600E detected in ctDNA without resistance mutations are retreated.
A KAIST-led study (Advanced Science, June 2026) used a single pre-surgery blood sample to analyze 18 circulating amino acids by a specialized method (19F NMR). It found a glycine-centered metabolic 'network' that shifts as colorectal cancer advances; a model combining this network with the standard CEA marker predicted recurrence better than CEA alone (independent fact-check: verified against the primary publication).
DIOPTRA protein biomarker study (Theme 3) also uses blood for early detection — complementary to ctDNA's strength in monitoring established disease.
ctDNA can detect molecular residual disease (MRD) [tiny amounts of cancer left after surgery] weeks to months before it shows up on scans, potentially allowing earlier action (general medical guidance).
Dynamic monitoring: ctDNA changes during therapy predict response/resistance earlier than imaging — e.g., KRAS mutant allele fraction rise signals anti-EGFR resistance.
Retreatment selection: BRAF V600E ctDNA clearance predicts durable benefit from encorafenib/cetuximab; re-emergence without resistance mutations suggests retreatment may work (BRICKET).
Amino acid network adds a metabolic layer: Reflects systemic host-tumor interaction (cachexia, anabolism), not just tumor DNA; may identify high-risk patients missed by ctDNA (e.g., low shed tumors).
Stage II/III colon cancer patients post-surgery: ctDNA (MRD) status guides adjuvant chemotherapy decision (DYNAMIC trial paradigm: ctDNA-negative → omit chemo; ctDNA-positive → intensify).
Metastatic CRC patients on targeted therapy (anti-EGFR, BRAF/EGFR inhibitors): Serial ctDNA for early resistance detection and retreatment decisions.
Patients with "indeterminate" imaging findings: ctDNA can clarify true progression vs pseudoprogression.
Patients where tissue biopsy is infeasible: Liquid biopsy provides molecular profiling (MSI, RAS, BRAF, HER2, NTRK) from blood.
All newly diagnosed CRC patients: Preoperative amino acid network profiling (if validated) could stratify recurrence risk beyond stage.

The Hope

  • Hope: a simple blood test could detect relapse months before scans, spare some patients unnecessary chemotherapy, and help match others to the right targeted therapy at the right time (general medical guidance).
  • Reality: ctDNA is powerful for monitoring but not perfect — false negatives occur in low-shedding tumors, assays are not standardized across labs, and acting on a positive result does not always have a proven treatment to offer (general medical guidance).

The Reality

  • Reality: insurance coverage and cost vary, and the best testing intervals and action thresholds are still being defined (general medical guidance).
  • Reality: the metabolic (amino-acid) approach is promising but early — it is years from routine use and would need prospective validation before changing care (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

ctDNA assays: both tumor-informed (designed from a patient's tumor) and tumor-naive tests are in use, with differing sensitivity; examples include several commercial MRD assays (general medical guidance).

PARERE (GONO): a trial using ctDNA to guide anti-EGFR (panitumumab) retreatment versus regorafenib sequencing in chemorefractory metastatic disease.

BRICKET (GONO): a trial using ctDNA to guide encorafenib/cetuximab retreatment in BRAF V600E metastatic disease, with data at ESMO 2026.

Established ctDNA-guided adjuvant trials (such as DYNAMIC and GALAXY) support using MRD status to guide chemotherapy decisions in earlier-stage colon cancer, with further trials ongoing (general medical guidance).

Amino-acid network assay (KAIST): a research-stage metabolic blood test combining 18 amino acids with CEA to predict recurrence risk.

Evidence Uncertainty

ctDNA assays vary: tumor-informed tests are generally more sensitive than tumor-naive ones, and detection depends on how much DNA a tumor sheds, so a negative result does not fully rule out residual disease (general medical guidance).

The adjuvant ctDNA strategy (acting on MRD status) is supported by trials like DYNAMIC in stage II colon cancer, but its role in stage III and in guiding treatment escalation is still being established (general medical guidance).

Retreatment trials (PARERE, BRICKET) are ongoing; they test whether ctDNA can reliably select patients for targeted retreatment, but results are not yet mature.

The KAIST amino-acid study is a single research report using pre-surgery samples; it shows an association with recurrence risk, not a validated clinical test, and needs confirmation in larger, independent groups.
