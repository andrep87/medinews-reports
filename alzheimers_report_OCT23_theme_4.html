Tau-Targeting Therapies and Precision Biomarkers Advance

"Tau — the protein that forms tangles and tracks closely with cognitive decline — is now a central treatment target, with several strategies in clinical testing: an antisense drug (diranersen) that lowers tau production, an oral muscarinic agonist (NBI-1117567) being tested to support cognition alongside standard drugs, and a newer blood test (brain-derived p-tau217) that can distinguish Alzheimer's tau from tau elevations caused by other conditions. Researchers also identified a splicing regulator (CELF2) that shifts the balance of tau forms."

CELIA Phase 2 trial (diranersen, a tau antisense oligonucleotide [a drug that lowers production of tau by targeting its genetic message]): more than 400 participants with mild cognitive impairment or mild Alzheimer's, given the drug into the spinal fluid over 76 weeks. Preliminary results suggested reduced tau markers and signs of cognitive benefit.
Neurocrine Biosciences started a Phase 2 trial of NBI-1117567, an oral muscarinic agonist [a drug that stimulates acetylcholine receptors involved in memory and attention], as an add-on to standard cholinesterase-inhibitor therapy in Alzheimer's.
Researchers at UT Health San Antonio identified CELF2, an RNA-binding protein that regulates how tau is assembled; their work links higher CELF2 activity to tau-related cognitive dysfunction, and reducing it shifted tau toward less harmful forms in models (independent fact-check: the draft said CELF2 knockout 'improved cognition'; the source describes CELF2 as driving cognitive dysfunction).
A separate study developed a two-step blood test (brain-derived p-tau217) that measures only brain-derived tau, avoiding false positives from muscle-derived tau in conditions such as ALS — helping confirm Alzheimer's even when other conditions are present.
Tau tangles track closely with cognitive decline, and anti-amyloid drugs do not directly stop tau spread, so targeting tau aims at a key driver of neurodegeneration (general medical guidance).
Diranersen lowers tau at the source (by degrading tau's genetic message); if proven safe and effective, this kind of approach could be relevant across tau-related diseases (general medical guidance).
NBI-1117567 is being tested to support cognition on top of disease-targeting therapy; its design aims for the memory-related receptors while limiting side effects seen with older drugs in this class (general medical guidance).
A brain-derived p-tau217 test addresses a real diagnostic problem: standard p-tau217 can be elevated by non-brain conditions, and isolating brain-derived tau improves accuracy for treatment decisions.
Diranersen: people with early symptomatic Alzheimer's (mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia); because it is given into the spinal fluid, it would initially be delivered at specialized centers.
NBI-1117567: people with mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's already on a stable cholinesterase inhibitor; it is oral and once daily, and was studied as an add-on (general medical guidance).
Future CELF2-based therapies: potentially relevant to tau-related diseases, but this is early research with no trials yet (general medical guidance).
Brain-derived p-tau217 testing: particularly useful for people with suspected Alzheimer's who also have conditions that can raise tau in the blood, and for research needing high accuracy.
If you have mild cognitive impairment or mild Alzheimer's, you may wish to ask your neurologist whether you are a candidate for tau-targeted trials such as CELIA (diranersen) or the NBI-1117567 trial (general medical guidance).
If you have ALS or a muscle disease along with memory concerns, you may wish to ask whether a brain-derived p-tau217 test is appropriate, since standard tests can be misleading in that situation (general medical guidance).
If you are on a cholinesterase inhibitor (such as donepezil, rivastigmine, or galantamine), you may wish to ask whether you could be eligible for the NBI-1117567 add-on trial (general medical guidance).
For future tau-splicing therapies, there are no trials yet; you may wish to ask your care team to keep watch for them (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • Hope: lowering tau (diranersen) or shifting it toward less harmful forms (CELF2) could slow decline, a cognitive add-on (NBI-1117567) could support daily function, and precise biomarkers could match the right patients to the right drugs (general medical guidance).
  • Reality: spinal-fluid delivery (diranersen) is invasive and limited to specialized centers; NBI-1117567, if it works, would support symptoms rather than stop the disease; and CELF2-based therapies are years away (general medical guidance).

The Reality

  • Brain-derived p-tau217 would initially be available mainly through reference labs, with higher cost and turnaround than standard tests, and guidance is needed on when to use it (general medical guidance).
  • A future of combination therapy (anti-amyloid plus anti-tau plus symptomatic support) raises questions of complexity, drug interactions, and cost (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Diranersen (tau-lowering antisense oligonucleotide): Phase 2 CELIA (more than 400 participants, 76 weeks, given into the spinal fluid). Preliminary data suggested tau reduction and a cognitive signal; full peer-reviewed results are awaited.

NBI-1117567 (Neurocrine): an oral muscarinic agonist; Phase 2 began in 2026 as an add-on to cholinesterase inhibitors, with cognitive performance as a focus. It is mechanistically distinct from amyloid- and tau-targeting drugs (general medical guidance).

CELF2 (splicing regulator): preclinical work showing it influences the balance of tau forms and also affects the amyloid precursor protein — a potential dual target, but far from the clinic (general medical guidance).

Brain-derived p-tau217 (two-step blood assay): validated analytically to separate brain-derived from muscle-derived tau; broader clinical validation and simpler versions are still needed.

Evidence Uncertainty

Diranersen: the Phase 2 data are preliminary, and full peer-reviewed results are pending; antisense drugs require repeated spinal-fluid dosing, and long-term safety of lowering tau is unknown.

NBI-1117567: the Phase 2 trial is just underway; showing a cognitive benefit on top of existing drugs is a high bar, and human safety data are still being gathered (general medical guidance).

CELF2: based on laboratory and animal work, with human brain-tissue validation ongoing; delivering splicing-modifying drugs to the brain is a significant challenge.

Brain-derived p-tau217: analytically strong, but clinical validation across diverse groups is needed, and the two-step method is complex, which may limit near-term routine use.

What To Watch

CELIA full results: peer-reviewed Phase 2 efficacy and safety data, and decisions about a Phase 3 trial (general medical guidance).

NBI-1117567 Phase 2 readout: cognitive and safety results versus placebo on top of standard therapy (general medical guidance).

CELF2 human validation: results from brain-tissue studies and any industry interest in tau-splicing approaches (general medical guidance).

Wider clinical adoption of brain-derived p-tau217 testing and how it is incorporated into practice guidelines (general medical guidance).
