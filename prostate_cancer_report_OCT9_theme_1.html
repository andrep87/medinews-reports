Five-Session Radiotherapy Shows Durable Cancer Control With Fewer Side Effects Than Surgery in Early Prostate Cancer

"The PACE-A phase 3 trial followed 123 men with localized, low-to-intermediate-risk prostate cancer for a median of 8 years after either 5-session SBRT or surgery. Cancer control was similar between the two, though the trial was small and not powered to prove equivalence. SBRT was associated with substantially less urinary incontinence and better sexual function."

The PACE-A phase 3 trial randomized 123 men with localized, low-to-intermediate-risk prostate cancer to either 5-session SBRT [stereotactic body radiotherapy — precisely targeted radiation] or radical prostatectomy [surgical removal of the prostate], and followed them for a median of 8 years. Hormone therapy was not permitted.
At 8 years, 91.2% of SBRT patients and 83.7% of surgery patients remained free of biochemical or clinical failure. With only 13 total events, the difference was not statistically significant (hazard ratio 0.48, 95% CI 0.16–1.46, p=0.18), and the trial was not powered to establish that one treatment matches the other.
At 5 years, 3 of 36 SBRT patients needed daily urinary pads versus 14 of 29 surgery patients. Sexual function scores declined in both groups but were higher after SBRT (median 19 vs 5 on a 25-point scale).
SBRT was delivered as 36.25 Gy in 5 treatments over 1–2 weeks. Surgery could be open, laparoscopic, or robot-assisted.
No patient in either arm had died of prostate cancer at 8 years.
This is the first phase 3 trial to directly compare 5-session SBRT with surgery for localized prostate cancer, providing long-term data on both cancer control and quality of life.
Cancer control was similar between the two approaches, while SBRT was associated with markedly less urinary pad use and better preserved sexual function.
The 5-session schedule is completed in 1–2 weeks of outpatient visits, which reduces treatment burden compared with surgery and recovery (general medical guidance).
Because the trial was small with few events, it supports SBRT as a reasonable option rather than proving the two treatments are equivalent.
Men with localized prostate cancer who were eligible for surgery — the trial enrolled men with clinical stage T1–T2, Gleason score 3+4 or lower, and PSA under 20 ng/mL (independent fact-check: these are the trial's stated eligibility criteria).
The trial population was predominantly intermediate-risk (about 94% intermediate-risk, 6% low-risk).
Men who prioritize preserving urinary continence and sexual function and prefer a shorter, non-invasive course.
This does not address men who need pelvic lymph node irradiation or hormone therapy, who were not part of this trial.
You may wish to ask your urologist and radiation oncologist how your expected cancer control and side-effect risks compare between SBRT and surgery for your specific situation.
Seeing both a surgeon and a radiation oncologist before deciding is the standard recommendation for localized prostate cancer (general medical guidance).
You may wish to ask whether validated quality-of-life questionnaires will be used to track urinary, bowel, and sexual function over time (general medical guidance).
If you are considering SBRT, you may wish to ask whether it is offered at your center or whether a referral is needed.

The Hope

  • Hope: Men can be treated with 5 outpatient sessions, keep their urinary and sexual function, and return to normal activities within weeks.
  • Reality: The difference in cancer control was not statistically significant, so this supports SBRT as an option rather than proving superiority. About 9% of SBRT patients still had cancer recurrence at 8 years.

The Reality

  • SBRT requires access to specialized radiotherapy equipment and expertise that is not available everywhere; travel may be needed (general medical guidance).
  • Both treatments carry some risk of urinary, bowel, or sexual side effects, though these differed in pattern and severity between the two approaches.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

SBRT (36.25 Gy in 5 fractions): Delivered over 1–2 weeks as an outpatient; compared directly with surgery in PACE-A.

Radical prostatectomy: Surgical removal of the prostate, which in this trial could be open, laparoscopic, or robot-assisted. It showed higher rates of urinary incontinence and sexual dysfunction.

PACE-C: A related trial comparing SBRT with moderately fractionated radiotherapy in higher-risk disease is ongoing (general medical guidance).

Earlier randomized trials comparing surgery with radiation used longer, conventionally fractionated radiation rather than 5-session SBRT.

Evidence Uncertainty

Strengths: Phase 3 randomized design, 8-year median follow-up, patient-reported quality-of-life outcomes, and the first direct comparison of 5-session SBRT with surgery.

Key limitation: Only 123 patients with 13 total cancer events — far too few to establish that the treatments are equivalent. The 8-year difference numerically favored SBRT but was not statistically significant.

The sexual-function comparison was analyzed by the treatment each man actually received rather than the treatment assigned, and 10 of 60 men assigned to surgery did not undergo it — a weaker method than the cancer-control analysis used (independent fact-check).

Generalizability to centers without specialized SBRT equipment and expertise is uncertain, and longer follow-up is still accruing (general medical guidance).

What To Watch

PACE-C results comparing SBRT with moderately fractionated radiotherapy in higher-risk disease.

Longer-term (beyond 10-year) PACE-A follow-up for late toxicity and overall survival.

Whether treatment guidelines incorporate the 8-year PACE-A data (general medical guidance).

Real-world outcomes in community settings outside specialized centers (general medical guidance).
