Breakthrough in Detecting a Hidden Cause of Hypertension — Continuous Hormone Monitoring Reveals Nighttime Aldosterone Surges Missed by Standard Tests

"New research shows that a common, treatable hormone disorder called primary aldosteronism [a condition where the adrenal glands make too much aldosterone, a hormone that raises blood pressure by holding onto salt and water] often goes undetected because standard blood tests miss nighttime hormone surges. A wearable device that tracks hormones every 20 minutes over 24 hours revealed these hidden bursts, especially during sleep, potentially changing how doctors diagnose and cure this form of high blood pressure."

Researchers used a wearable device (U-RHYTHM) about the size of a mobile phone that attaches at the waist to continuously sample hormones through the skin every 20 minutes for 24 hours while 60 patients went about normal activities, including sleeping at home.
They discovered that in primary aldosteronism, aldosterone [the hormone that controls salt and water balance] does not stay constantly high. Instead, it surges in bursts, particularly at night, then drops back to near-normal levels by morning.
These nighttime bursts were most pronounced in patients whose condition was caused by a problem in just one adrenal gland [unilateral disease], and the abnormal pattern disappeared after that gland was surgically removed.
Standard single blood tests taken during the day can miss these surges entirely, leading to delayed or missed diagnosis of a condition that affects up to 1 in 5 people with high blood pressure and increases risk of heart disease, stroke, and diabetes.
Primary aldosteronism is the most common curable cause of high blood pressure, but current guidelines recommend screening all hypertensive patients — yet diagnosis is often delayed or never made because hormone levels fluctuate and single tests catch the wrong moment.
If undetected, excess aldosterone damages the heart and blood vessels beyond just raising blood pressure, increasing cardiovascular risk even when blood pressure appears controlled on medication.
Continuous 24-hour hormone monitoring could transform diagnosis from a single snapshot to a dynamic profile, catching nighttime bursts that reveal the disease and allowing earlier, targeted treatment — including potential surgical cure for unilateral cases.
This approach may also uncover milder or earlier forms of the disorder that current thresholds miss, opening doors to prevention of long-term complications.
Adults with high blood pressure that is difficult to control on multiple medications (resistant hypertension).
Patients diagnosed with hypertension at a young age (under 40) or with a family history of early-onset high blood pressure or adrenal disorders.
Anyone with low potassium levels (hypokalemia) [low blood potassium, which can cause muscle weakness, fatigue, or irregular heartbeats] on routine blood work, even if mild.
People with an adrenal nodule [a small growth on the adrenal gland] found incidentally on imaging (CT or MRI) done for another reason.
Ask your doctor: “Should I be screened for primary aldosteronism?” — especially if your blood pressure remains above target despite 2–3 medications, or if you have ever had low potassium.
Request a morning blood draw for aldosterone and renin [an enzyme from the kidneys that helps regulate blood pressure] ratio (ARR) after sitting quietly for 30 minutes, and confirm with your doctor whether any medications need to be paused beforehand (some drugs falsely raise or lower the ratio).
If your ARR is elevated, ask for confirmatory testing (such as saline infusion or captopril challenge) rather than relying on a single screening test.
Keep a home blood pressure log (twice daily, morning and evening, for 7 days) and bring it to your next visit — patterns of higher morning readings may hint at aldosterone-driven hypertension.

The Hope

  • Hope: A curable cause of hypertension may be hiding in plain sight; wearable monitoring could turn a “missed diagnosis” into a “found and fixed” one for millions; surgical cure means no lifelong pills for some.
  • Reality: The U-RHYTHM device is still investigational — not available in clinics today. Standard screening (ARR) remains the gateway, and it has its own limitations. Surgery requires specialized centers. Mineralocorticoid antagonists (such as spironolactone or eplerenone) are effective but can cause side effects including high potassium, breast tenderness, or menstrual changes (general medical guidance).

The Reality

  • Access: Even after diagnosis, not all patients live near endocrine hypertension specialists or surgeons experienced in adrenalectomy; insurance may not cover advanced testing until guidelines formally endorse 24-hour monitoring.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

If confirmed, primary aldosteronism caused by one adrenal gland (aldosterone-producing adenoma) can potentially be cured by minimally invasive surgical removal of that gland (adrenalectomy).

For bilateral adrenal hyperplasia [both glands overactive] or patients not suitable for surgery, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists [medications like spironolactone or eplerenone that block aldosterone’s effects] are effective and reduce cardiovascular risk beyond blood pressure lowering.

The U-RHYTHM wearable technology (developed at University of Bristol, commercialized by Dynamic Therapeutics) is in early validation studies; larger trials are needed before it becomes a routine clinical tool.

Research is ongoing to define the best clinical pathways using dynamic hormone measurement and to develop simpler, automated analysis of 24-hour hormone profiles for widespread use.

Evidence Uncertainty

Strengths: First study to capture 24-hour aldosterone rhythms in real-world conditions (n=60, multi-center); used objective wearable sampling every 20 minutes; confirmed surges linked to unilateral disease; pattern resolved after adrenalectomy — strong mechanistic evidence.

Limitations: Small proof-of-concept sample (60 patients); not yet a randomized diagnostic trial; device not yet FDA/EMA approved for clinical use; no data yet on cost-effectiveness or false-positive/false-negative rates in broader populations.

Uncertain: Whether nighttime surges occur in all subtypes of primary aldosteronism; optimal sampling frequency and duration for clinical deployment; how to integrate dynamic profiles into existing guideline algorithms.

What To Watch

Larger prospective trials validating U-RHYTHM or similar continuous monitoring against clinical outcomes (diagnostic yield, time to treatment, cardiovascular events).

Endocrine Society guideline updates on whether dynamic hormone profiling should supplement or replace single-time-point screening for primary aldosteronism.

Development of simplified, lower-cost wearable or home-collection kits for 24-hour hormone assessment.

Studies linking specific nighttime surge patterns to cardiovascular risk stratification and treatment response prediction.
