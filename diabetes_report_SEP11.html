Reprogramming the Pancreas: Gene Editing and Menin Inhibition Offer Paths to Regrow Insulin-Producing Cells

"Researchers have discovered two distinct molecular 'brakes' — the ALDH3B2 gene and the menin protein — that keep pancreatic duct cells from becoming insulin-producing beta cells [the cells that make insulin]. Disabling either brake in human cells triggers their transformation into functional beta-like cells that normalize blood sugar in diabetic mice, opening a potential path to regenerate a patient's own insulin supply without transplants or lifelong immunosuppression [drugs that weaken the immune system to prevent rejection]."

Harvard researchers (Joslin Diabetes Center) used a genome-wide CRISPR screen [a technique that tests thousands of genes at once] across 19,000 genes in human pancreatic duct cells and identified ALDH3B2 as a master switch that locks duct cells in their default identity. Silencing it increased the conversion of duct cells to beta-like cells from under 1% to about 8.5%.
The reprogrammed cells began producing insulin. They lost a chemical marker (DNA methylation) on the insulin gene that had been keeping it switched off, passed through a temporary progenitor-like state, and matured into glucose-responsive beta-like cells.
Separately, Kura Oncology spun out Caspian Therapeutics with $50 million in funding (led by BVF Partners, joined by Eli Lilly and others) to develop KO-7246, an oral menin inhibitor [a drug that blocks the menin protein]. Menin is a scaffold protein that prevents beta cells from multiplying. Note: T1D Fund involvement was reported by some outlets but could not be independently confirmed in all press releases.
In multiple animal models of Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes, blocking menin expanded functional beta-cell mass, increased the body's own insulin production, improved blood sugar control, and — notably — the effects persisted even after treatment stopped, suggesting durable regeneration.
Current diabetes care replaces insulin (injections, pumps) or transplants donor or stem-cell beta cells — both limited by supply, cost, and the need for lifelong immunosuppression to prevent rejection.
These approaches aim to unlock a patient's own abundant pancreatic duct cells, converting them into insulin factories from within — potentially avoiding immune rejection and donor shortages entirely.
ALDH3B2 is an enzyme, so a future drug could block it with a pill (not just gene editing). Researchers already saw similar conversion using DEAB, a broad ALDH-blocking chemical.
Menin inhibitors are already FDA-approved for leukemia (ziftomenib/Komzifti), providing a known safety framework. Caspian's KO-7246 is designed specifically for metabolic disease with a potentially better safety profile (no low blood sugar episodes in preclinical testing).
Ultimately, anyone with Type 1 diabetes (autoimmune beta-cell destruction) or Type 2 diabetes (progressive beta-cell loss) who still has pancreatic duct cells — which is nearly all patients (general medical guidance).
Early applications would likely target recently diagnosed patients with some remaining duct-cell flexibility, or those facing transplant waitlists or immunosuppression risks (general medical guidance).
The research is preclinical (lab and animal studies only); no human trials have begun. Caspian aims for regulatory filing 'as soon as practicable' with Phase I data expected around 2028.

Gestational Diabetes Is a Cardiometabolic Emergency — Not a Pregnancy-Only Condition

"Gestational diabetes (GDM) [diabetes that develops during pregnancy] is not a temporary pregnancy complication — it marks women for a roughly 10-fold higher risk of Type 2 diabetes, 5-fold higher prediabetes risk, and significantly elevated high blood pressure, high cholesterol, obesity, and cardiovascular disease within just 2–3 years after delivery. Published data from over 1 million women, plus a Danish registry study awaiting full peer-reviewed publication, show Type 2 diabetes is diagnosed roughly 13 years earlier in women with prior GDM, and 40% are diagnosed before age 40."

A Stanford-led analysis of 1,153,998 commercially insured US women (median follow-up 2.4 years), published in JAMA Network Open: gestational diabetes was linked to 10 times higher risk of Type 2 diabetes (aHR 10.02), 5 times higher risk of prediabetes (5.32), 2.7 times higher risk of metabolic syndrome (2.72), 2 times higher risk of high cholesterol (2.00), 1.8 times higher risk of high blood pressure (1.76), 1.8 times higher risk of obesity (1.75), and 1.3 times higher risk of heart disease (1.28). Kidney disease risk was not increased (aHR 0.96).
The extra cardiometabolic risk appeared within the same window as routine postpartum glucose screening (the first few years after birth), not decades later. Type 2 diabetes risk stayed over 6-fold higher even 5–10 years after delivery.
A Danish registry study (DD2 cohort, n=2,865 women with prior pregnancy) — still an unpublished conference finding set to be presented at EASD 2026 (Sept 28–Oct 2), not yet peer-reviewed: women with prior GDM (n=173) were diagnosed with Type 2 diabetes at median age 43.5 vs. 56.3 years (almost 13 years earlier); about 40% were under age 40 at diagnosis vs. 6% without GDM. The GDM group used insulin twice as often at diagnosis (10.4% vs. 5.2%) and had lower C-peptide [a marker of insulin production].
A Japanese study (n=470): High-intensity breastfeeding (at least 80% breast milk) was linked to 75% lower odds of high triglycerides and 73% lower odds of an unhealthy cholesterol ratio at 6–9 weeks after birth, regardless of insulin sensitivity.
GDM acts like a 'failed stress test' — pregnancy reveals hidden heart and metabolism vulnerability that persists and worsens after delivery.
Current guidelines treat the postpartum visit as a glucose check only. These data suggest it should become a comprehensive cardiometabolic visit: blood pressure, cholesterol, weight, glucose, and kidney function.
Earlier Type 2 diabetes onset means longer disease duration, more complications, and higher lifetime costs (general medical guidance).
Breastfeeding intensity is a modifiable factor that improves early postpartum cholesterol and triglyceride levels — a rare actionable intervention in this time window.
All women with a history of GDM (affects roughly 1 in 7 pregnancies globally, about 300,000 per year in the US).
Particularly urgent for women with additional risk factors: South Asian/Asian American ethnicity (higher GDM prevalence and progression risk), Medicaid/uninsured status (structural barriers to postpartum care), prior preeclampsia, or family history of diabetes/heart disease.
Women with borderline glucose levels that don't quite meet GDM diagnostic thresholds also showed 47% higher adverse pregnancy odds and faster fetal growth — suggesting even sub-GDM blood sugar elevation carries risk.
If you had GDM: Schedule a comprehensive cardiometabolic visit now (not just glucose) — request blood pressure, lipid panel [cholesterol test], weight, HbA1c [average blood sugar over 3 months], and kidney function tests (eGFR, UACR). Repeat annually.
Ask your provider: 'Am I up to date on the National Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP)?' DPP lifestyle coaching or metformin has been shown to reduce Type 2 diabetes progression in women with a GDM history.
If pregnant or postpartum: Aim for high-intensity breastfeeding (at least 80% breast milk) if feasible — it's linked to a better early cholesterol profile. Request lactation support if needed.
If planning pregnancy: Discuss optimizing weight, blood pressure, and cholesterol with your OB/GYN or primary care before conceiving — earlier intervention may change the long-term trajectory (general medical guidance).
Track your numbers: Keep a personal record of postpartum HbA1c, blood pressure, cholesterol, and weight. Share with any new provider — the handoff from obstetrics to primary care is a major gap where information gets lost.

Advanced Diabetes Technology Alone Cannot Close the Equity Gap

"Providing continuous glucose monitors (CGM) [wearable sensors that track blood sugar] and insulin pumps does not eliminate the blood-sugar disparities driven by social and economic circumstances. In a study of 1,743 CGM users, Black race was independently associated with higher HbA1c [average blood sugar], and public insurance was independently associated with less time in a healthy blood-sugar range and more time in dangerous high blood sugar — even with identical technology access. A significant interaction effect was found specifically among Black children for higher average sensor glucose readings. Equity requires pairing devices with education, behavioral support, and structural interventions."

JAMA Network Open study (Williamson et al.): 1,743 adults and children with Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes using CGM (with or without a pump). Black race was independently associated with 0.55% higher HbA1c. A significant interaction effect was found specifically among Black children for higher average sensor glucose (+17.88 mg/dL). Public insurance was independently associated with 0.26% higher HbA1c, 3.56% less time-in-range [time blood sugar stays in a healthy zone], 3.49% more time-above-range [time blood sugar is dangerously high], and a 4.5-point higher glycemic risk index [an overall measure of blood sugar danger]. Living in a more economically disadvantaged neighborhood was linked to 3.26% more glucose variability [day-to-day blood sugar swings].
NIH-funded Utah study (ARCHES, about 3 million women, 99,978 with endometriosis): Endometriosis [a condition where uterine-like tissue grows outside the uterus] was linked to 46% higher Type 2 diabetes risk overall. Risk more than doubled for extra-pelvic endometriosis [tissue found outside the pelvis]. Link was strongest in premenopausal women without obesity (BMI under 30) — a group usually considered low-risk for diabetes.
Brazilian Indigenous study (946 people in Tupiniquim and Guarani communities): Despite tropical sun exposure, vitamin D deficiency (below 30 ng/mL) was independently linked to 77% higher Type 2 diabetes odds; each 1 ng/mL increase in vitamin D was linked to about 4% lower odds.
Technology access does not equal technology benefit. CGM and pumps generate data, but acting on that data requires literacy, time, stable housing, food security, and clinical support — all unevenly distributed (general medical guidance).
The endometriosis finding reveals a new, inflammation-driven metabolic risk in women without traditional risk factors (obesity, older age), suggesting current diabetes screening misses a vulnerable group.
Vitamin D deficiency in a sun-rich environment shows that biology (skin pigmentation, genetics, inflammation) and behavior (time spent indoors, diet) can matter more than latitude — routine screening and supplementation could be a low-cost way to reduce disparities (general medical guidance).
These findings are not about patients failing to 'comply' — they point to systemic gaps in addressing the real-world context in which people live and use technology (general medical guidance).
CGM/pump users: Especially those with public insurance (Medicaid/Medicare), Black patients, and residents of economically disadvantaged neighborhoods — disparities persisted even after accounting for pump use and other demographics.
Women with endometriosis: Particularly premenopausal and not obese (BMI under 30) — current guidelines don't flag this group for early metabolic screening.
Indigenous people, people with more skin pigmentation, and those who spend a lot of time indoors: Vitamin D deficiency is common even in sunny climates, and the associated metabolic risk is independent of traditional factors.
Clinicians and insurers: Prescribing technology without also addressing social and economic needs (food, housing, health literacy, support) wastes resources and widens outcome gaps (general medical guidance).

Ask about a social-needs screening (food, housing, transportation) — many clinics now have patient navigators (general medical guidance).
If you have endometriosis (especially if premenopausal and not obese): Ask your OB/GYN or primary care doctor for an annual HbA1c, cholesterol panel, and blood pressure check — 'I have endometriosis, which research links to earlier Type 2 diabetes risk. Can we screen proactively?'
Ask about vitamin D testing: 'Given my diabetes risk, can we check my 25-hydroxyvitamin D level?' If it's low (below 30 ng/mL), discuss supplementation with your doctor (general medical guidance).

Once-Weekly Insulin, App-Controlled Pumps, and Safer SGLT2 Use — What's Actionable Now

"Three practical changes are reaching patients: once-weekly basal insulin (icodec, brand name Awiqli, FDA-approved March 26, 2026) cuts injections from 365 to 52 per year but requires a careful one-time dose adjustment and strict missed-dose rules; MiniMed Flex — already FDA-cleared and launched in the US since mid-2026, and now also CE-Marked in Europe — is the first smartphone-app-controlled insulin pump with 80% time-in-range in real-world use; and a 322,597-episode Scandinavian study shows SGLT2 inhibitor ketoacidosis risk persists throughout treatment (not just at start), meaning ongoing risk checks and 'sick-day' pausing of the drug during illness matter."

Insulin icodec (Awiqli, Novo Nordisk): FDA-approved on March 26, 2026 for adults with Type 2 diabetes only, with nationwide US launch in the second half of 2026. The ONWARDS clinical trials showed it controls blood sugar as well as daily basal insulin. A separate trial in Type 1 diabetes (ONWARDS 6) showed more severe low blood sugar episodes, so Type 1 is excluded. Insulin efsitora alfa (Lilly) is in the QWINT Phase 3 program, not yet approved.
MiniMed Flex (from MiniMed, spun out of Medtronic): FDA-cleared March 18, 2026 and launched in the US in June 2026 — its first market. It then received CE Mark for Europe in September 2026, with a European launch expected around November 2026. In the US it's approved for Type 1 diabetes ages 7+ and insulin-requiring Type 2 in adults; the European version covers ages 2+ (Type 1 and Type 2) and use during pregnancy. About half the size of the prior MiniMed 780G, holds 300 units of insulin, controlled entirely from a smartphone app, uses an algorithm (SmartGuard) that auto-adjusts insulin about every 5 minutes, includes Meal Detection technology, and works with the Simplera Sync and Instinct (Abbott-made) glucose sensors. Real-world data show users spend about 80% of their time in a healthy glucose range with recommended settings.
SGLT2 inhibitor ketoacidosis [a dangerous buildup of acids called ketones in the blood] (Karolinska-led study, Sweden/Denmark/Norway): 322,597 treatment episodes among 282,282 patients with Type 2 diabetes, 1,452 DKA events (2.43 per 1,000 person-years), published in The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology. Risk was highest in the first roughly 180 days but persisted throughout treatment. Risk factors included high HbA1c (≥83 mmol/mol or 9.7%: 15× odds), low BMI (<20: roughly 10×), prior DKA (>10×), malnutrition (>10×), recent low blood sugar (>5×). Infection was the most common trigger (in 31.8% of DKA cases, OR 7.6). After a DKA episode, only 25% stayed on their SGLT2 drug; insulin use jumped from 32% to 73%.
Dapagliflozin + insulin (small RCT, n=86, 3 months): Combination reduced HbA1c by 24.9% vs. 10.3% with insulin alone; fasting glucose fell more (-23.6% vs. -5.6%); weight dropped more (-5.4% vs. -3.3%); blood pressure improved; cholesterol improved (total cholesterol -35%, LDL -15.7%, HDL +16.3%). Adverse events were 71.4% lower; no DKA occurred.
Once-weekly insulin dramatically reduces injection burden — but only for Type 2 diabetes. Switching requires a one-time 'loading dose' (not simply the daily dose multiplied by seven). The missed-dose window is 4 days maximum. Pharmacists need to watch for mix-ups with other weekly injectable drugs (like semaglutide or dulaglutide) (general medical guidance).
The app-controlled pump moves pump interaction to a phone — more discreet, potentially less stigma, and easier for caregivers to help remotely. Meal Detection catches missed meal doses. Because MiniMed Flex launched in the US months before its European approval, US patients and doctors have a head start building real-world experience. It is still not 'set and forget' — sensors and infusion sites still need regular changes, and alarms still need a response (general medical guidance).
Doctors often only assess ketoacidosis risk with SGLT2 drugs when a patient starts treatment. This data shows the risk is ongoing and driven by factors that can change over time (blood sugar control, weight, illness). 'Sick-day' pausing of the SGLT2 drug during acute illness is evidence-based but widely underused.
The dapagliflozin + insulin trial challenges the common approach of simply increasing insulin doses when blood sugar isn't controlled — adding an SGLT2 drug may offer broader benefits (blood sugar, weight, blood pressure, cholesterol) with fewer side effects.
Insulin icodec: Adults with Type 2 diabetes on basal insulin (or starting insulin for the first time) — not for Type 1 diabetes, not during pregnancy, limited data in advanced kidney disease. Best suited to people who dislike daily injections and have a stable weekly routine (general medical guidance).
MiniMed Flex: People with Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes on insulin seeking a smaller, phone-controlled pump — now available in the US (since June 2026) and rolling out in Europe (from around November 2026). The European version is also approved for use during pregnancy. Requires a compatible sensor (Simplera Sync or Instinct) and a smartphone.
SGLT2 ketoacidosis vigilance: Anyone taking an SGLT2 inhibitor (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin, ertugliflozin) — especially those with poorly controlled blood sugar, low body weight, a prior DKA episode, malnutrition, frequent low blood sugar, or recurring infections.
Dapagliflozin + insulin: People with Type 2 diabetes on insulin who aren't at their blood sugar goal — particularly those who are also overweight, have high blood pressure, high cholesterol, or kidney disease, where SGLT2 drugs have proven benefits (general medical guidance).
If on daily basal insulin for Type 2 diabetes: Ask your provider, 'Am I a candidate for once-weekly icodec, which is now available in the US? Can we do the dose-conversion process together with pharmacist support?'
If considering a pump: Ask your diabetes team, 'Is MiniMed Flex available to me?' — it's already on the US market, and European patients should ask about the local launch timeline following the September 2026 approval. Also ask how Meal Detection works if you miss a meal dose.
If on an SGLT2 inhibitor: Keep a 'sick-day' card that says: 'If I am acutely ill (vomiting, diarrhea, fever, or unable to eat), I will STOP my SGLT2 inhibitor and check for ketones.' Ask your pharmacist to review this with you at every refill.
If on insulin and not at your target: Ask, 'Could we add dapagliflozin or a similar SGLT2 drug instead of just increasing my insulin dose?' Discuss the trial data showing larger blood sugar reduction plus weight, blood pressure, and cholesterol benefits with a lower adverse-event rate.
If on an SGLT2 inhibitor plus insulin, or have Type 1 diabetes: Ask your doctor about glucose-ketone combination sensors, which can flag rising ketone levels early.

MiniMed Flex + SmartGuard: An automated insulin-delivery system that adjusts basal insulin automatically while meal doses are entered manually. Meal Detection provides auto-correction for missed meals. FDA-cleared and US-launched first (March/June 2026); CE-Marked for Europe second (September 2026).

Dapagliflozin (Farxiga): An SGLT2 inhibitor, now available as a generic in some markets. FDA-approved for Type 2 diabetes, heart failure, and chronic kidney disease (general medical guidance).

Oral insulin (Endo Axiom, Sydney): Early-phase research using enzyme-responsive, liver-targeted nanoparticles [tiny particles designed to deliver insulin through the digestive system]. Phase 1a in healthy volunteers. Phase 1b in Type 1 diabetes planned. Over 20 years of research in this area.

Evidence Uncertainty

Icodec: ONWARDS program (roughly 2,700–3,500 adults with Type 2 diabetes across component trials); non-inferior HbA1c, similar severe low blood sugar rates vs. daily basal. FDA approval confirmed for March 26, 2026. Uncertain: long-term (beyond 1 year) safety, real-world adherence to a weekly schedule, cost/access, Type 1 diabetes contraindication.

MiniMed Flex: Real-world 80% time-in-range with recommended settings. FDA clearance (March 2026) and US launch (June 2026) preceded the European CE Mark (September 2026) — these are sequential milestones, not simultaneous. Uncertain: randomized trial data, performance in diverse populations (children, older adults, people with lower health literacy), pump failure rates.

SGLT2 DKA: Large, real-world registry study (322,597 treatment episodes, 1,452 events), published in a peer-reviewed journal. Uncertain: causality (confounding by indication), generalizability outside Scandinavia, optimal HbA1c threshold for pausing, restart criteria after illness.

Dapagliflozin + insulin: Randomized trial but small (n=86), 3 months, open-label, single site (Brazil). Uncertain: durability of benefit, hard outcomes (heart disease, kidney disease), generalizability, optimal insulin dose reduction.

What To Watch

Icodec real-world evidence: Post-marketing surveillance for dose-conversion errors, adherence, off-label use in Type 1 diabetes, and cost-effectiveness.

Efsitora alfa Phase 3 readouts (2026–2027): Head-to-head vs. icodec? Type 1 diabetes data? FDA submission timeline.

MiniMed Flex European launch: Expected around November 2026 following the September CE Mark; watch for real-world European data alongside the already-available US experience.

SGLT2 sick-day protocol standardization: ADA/EASD/KDIGO guideline updates on when to pause and restart; pharmacy-led sick-day counseling mandates.

Dapagliflozin + insulin guideline inclusion: Will SGLT2 add-on become preferred over increasing insulin doses in future ADA/EASD algorithms?

Oral insulin Phase 1b (Type 1 diabetes): Safety, dosing, and whether this delivery approach can work in actual diabetes patients.
