Platform Trials Redesign How We Test Alzheimer's Drugs

"Two major platform trials—AD-SMART in the UK and an ARPA-H-funded US initiative led by Mass General Brigham and UC San Diego—are launching to test multiple repurposed and novel therapies simultaneously using shared infrastructure and blood biomarkers. This cancer-trial-inspired model aims to cut years off traditional one-drug-at-a-time development."

AD-SMART (UK): a Phase III multi-arm adaptive platform trial, sponsored by University College London, testing multiple Alzheimer's treatments at once. The first two arms are repurposed drugs with known safety profiles: immediate-release metformin (a diabetes drug) and atomoxetine (an ADHD drug). Participants are randomized to standard care plus placebo, metformin, or atomoxetine; the target is about 1,200 patients aged 55+ with Alzheimer's or mixed dementia (independent fact-check: sponsor corrected to UCL per the trial registry).
ARPA-H platform trial (US): up to $41 million over four years to Mass General Brigham (lead), with the UC San Diego Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study (ADCS) as clinical-trials coordinator. It is designed to test several regimens at once (repurposed drugs, natural products, and AI-identified combinations) and to use blood biomarkers (chiefly p-tau217) to detect treatment effects early.
Both trials use adaptive designs: ineffective arms can be dropped and new arms added, with a shared placebo group and common protocol. The approach is modeled on MND-SMART, an ALS platform trial that evaluated its first drugs in roughly three years rather than the usual eight-plus.
The overall goal is to make Alzheimer's drug testing faster and cheaper by sharing infrastructure across many candidate therapies.
Traditional Alzheimer's trials test one drug at a time over many years, and most fail. Platform trials test several drugs in parallel, share control arms, and use biomarkers as earlier readouts (general medical guidance).
Repurposed drugs such as metformin and atomoxetine already have years of safety data and low cost; if effective, they could reach patients faster. Their possible benefits in Alzheimer's are being tested, not yet proven (general medical guidance).
Using blood biomarkers to confirm participants have active Alzheimer's changes can make trials more efficient, and early biomarker changes may help trials make faster go/no-go decisions (general medical guidance).
This model builds reusable national trial infrastructure that future candidate drugs can plug into.
AD-SMART: people aged 55+ with a confirmed diagnosis of Alzheimer's or mixed (Alzheimer's plus vascular) dementia, willing to take an oral drug and attend in-person visits, living near a participating UK/NHS site.
ARPA-H platform: cognitively unimpaired or mildly impaired adults with biomarker evidence of early Alzheimer's, recruited across US sites through the ADCS network — a focus on earlier intervention than AD-SMART.
Both trials: people willing to be randomly assigned to an active drug or placebo; those on stable symptomatic medications (such as cholinesterase inhibitors or memantine) may still be eligible (general medical guidance).
Not for: advanced dementia, other neurodegenerative diagnoses (such as pure frontotemporal or Lewy body dementia), or people unable to undergo the required tests (general medical guidance).
UK residents aged 55+ with Alzheimer's or mixed dementia may wish to ask their memory clinic about AD-SMART eligibility, or register interest through the UK's Join Dementia Research service (general medical guidance).
US residents with mild cognitive impairment or early Alzheimer's may wish to ask their neurologist whether they could be screened for the ARPA-H platform trial, including whether a p-tau217 blood test is appropriate (general medical guidance).
You may wish to join the Alzheimer's Association TrialMatch service and note interest in platform or prevention trials to be notified when sites open (general medical guidance).
If you already take metformin or atomoxetine for another condition, you may wish to discuss it with your prescriber, but should not start or stop either drug for Alzheimer's on your own — their benefit in Alzheimer's is unproven (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • Hope: testing many drugs at once, using repurposed agents with known safety, and reading out biomarkers early could speed progress, with infrastructure reusable for years (general medical guidance).
  • Reality: first results are years away (AD-SMART around 2030; ARPA-H biomarker readouts around 2027-2028), and most tested drugs will likely fail despite strong rationale.

The Reality

  • Access: trial participation requires proximity to sites (initially limited), and participants may receive placebo; if a repurposed drug eventually works, off-label use could precede formal approval (general medical guidance).
  • Funding is time-limited, so long-term sustainability depends on continued government, academic, and industry support (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

AD-SMART arm 1 — metformin: a diabetes drug being tested for possible effects on brain inflammation and metabolism; taken orally, with well-known side effects (such as GI upset and, long-term, vitamin B12 lowering) (general medical guidance).

AD-SMART arm 2 — atomoxetine: an ADHD drug (a norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor) being tested for possible effects on attention and neuroprotection; taken orally, with known effects on blood pressure, heart rate, and sleep (general medical guidance).

ARPA-H platform: regimens are drawn from repurposed FDA-approved drugs, natural products, and AI-identified combinations, prioritized by laboratory data, safety, and mechanism (general medical guidance).

MND-SMART precedent: this ALS platform trial evaluated several drugs far faster than traditional trials, demonstrating the model works for neurodegeneration (though its first drugs were not effective).

Evidence Uncertainty

AD-SMART: designed around cognitive and quality-of-life endpoints over 18 months, with an adaptive design that allows early stopping of arms for futility or success; the roughly 1,200-patient size is intended to detect modest effects.

ARPA-H: a four-year effort aiming to generate biomarker and safety data for several regimens and identify the strongest for later confirmatory trials; the main biomarker is plasma p-tau217.

MND-SMART showed the platform design is feasible and faster per drug, but its first drugs failed — the design improves efficiency, not the odds any single drug works.

A key open question is whether biomarker changes (such as p-tau217 reduction) will reliably predict real cognitive benefit (general medical guidance).

What To Watch

AD-SMART: early safety and biomarker analyses, the addition of new drug arms, and expansion to more UK sites (general medical guidance).

ARPA-H platform: site activation across US locations, the first regimen selections, and standardization of the p-tau217 assay across sites (general medical guidance).

Lessons from MND-SMART as new drugs enter that platform, informing AD-SMART operations (general medical guidance).

Whether biomarker-based endpoints gain regulatory acceptance as a basis for faster decisions in prevention trials (general medical guidance).
