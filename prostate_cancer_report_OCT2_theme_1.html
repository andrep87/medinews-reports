Five-Treatment Radiation (SBRT) Matches Surgery for Localized Prostate Cancer with Better Urinary and Sexual Function

"A major phase 3 trial shows that a condensed, five-session radiation course (SBRT) controls localized prostate cancer just as well as surgery over eight years, but with far fewer men needing urinary pads and better preserved sexual function. This gives men a proven non-surgical curative option that is faster, less disruptive, and easier on daily life."

The PACE-A trial randomly assigned 123 men with low- or intermediate-risk localized prostate cancer to either surgical removal of the prostate or five-session SBRT [stereotactic body radiation therapy — high-dose, precisely targeted radiation given in 5 outpatient visits over 1-2 weeks]. No hormone therapy was used in either group.
At a median follow-up of 8 years, cancer control was similar: 91% of SBRT patients and 84% of surgery patients remained free of biochemical or clinical failure [rising PSA, visible recurrence, spread, or death from prostate cancer]. Only 13 failures occurred in total, and the difference was not statistically significant.
Urinary continence was substantially better with SBRT: 8% of SBRT patients used at least one urinary pad at 5 years, versus 48% after surgery.
Sexual function scores (on a 25-point scale where higher is better) were significantly better with SBRT at 5 years — median 19 versus 5 after surgery. Bowel side effects were low in both groups.
For decades, men had to choose between surgery and weeks of conventional radiation without head-to-head randomized data comparing modern, high-precision SBRT to modern robotic surgery; PACE-A fills that evidence gap.
Cancer control is excellent with both, so the decision increasingly hinges on quality of life: SBRT avoids general anesthesia, surgical incisions, catheterization, and prolonged recovery, and it preserves urinary and sexual function far better.
The 5-session course is completed in 1-2 weeks, compared with longer conventional radiation schedules or weeks of surgical recovery — reducing travel, time off work, and overall treatment burden.
These results support SBRT as a standard curative option for favorable intermediate-risk disease, and they reinforce that patients should meet with both a surgeon and a radiation oncologist before deciding.
Men with localized prostate cancer, clinical stage T1c-T2c, Gleason score 3+4 or lower, and PSA of 20 ng/mL or less who are fit for surgery — these were the trial's eligibility criteria.
The trial population was predominantly intermediate-risk, so results apply most directly to men with favorable intermediate-risk disease.
Men with high-risk disease were not studied here and typically need hormone therapy added to radiation — a different discussion (general medical guidance).
Men who prioritize preserving urinary continence and sexual function and want a shorter, non-invasive course are particularly strong candidates for SBRT.
Ask your urologist and radiation oncologist: 'Am I a candidate for 5-session SBRT, and how do my expected urinary and sexual outcomes compare with surgery in my specific case?'
Request a consultation with a radiation oncologist before finalizing any treatment plan — do not assume surgery is the only curative option. Seeing both specialists is the standard recommendation for localized disease (general medical guidance).
Confirm your cancer has been staged with MRI — all PACE-A participants were MRI-staged — and ask your team whether any additional imaging is appropriate for your risk level.
If you choose SBRT, ask your radiation oncologist about measures to protect the rectum and about your PSA monitoring schedule afterward (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • Hope: A 1-2 week outpatient treatment can control localized prostate cancer effectively while letting most men stay continent and sexually active.
  • Reality: SBRT is not risk-free — some men develop urinary frequency, urgency, or mild bowel changes. Erectile function declined in both groups, just substantially less with SBRT.

The Reality

  • Important caveat: With only 13 cancer events, this trial cannot definitively establish that one treatment controls cancer better than the other. It does provide strong evidence on quality-of-life differences.
  • Access: Not all centers offer SBRT. Check with your insurer about coverage and be prepared to travel to a center that offers it (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

SBRT (5 sessions): Delivers high-dose, precisely focused radiation to the prostate across 5 outpatient visits, typically over 1-2 weeks (general medical guidance).

Radical prostatectomy: Surgical removal of the prostate. PACE-A allowed open, laparoscopic, or robotic approaches. It remains a standard curative option, but showed higher rates of urinary incontinence and erectile dysfunction in this trial.

PACE-B: A larger companion trial comparing SBRT with conventionally fractionated radiation (not surgery), which demonstrated SBRT was non-inferior at 5 years.

PACE platform continues: Other cohorts are evaluating SBRT combined with hormone therapy for higher-risk disease, which could expand who is eligible.

Evidence Uncertainty

Strengths: Randomized phase 3 design, 8-year median follow-up, patient-reported outcomes as co-primary endpoints, and MRI staging for all participants.

Major limitation: Only 123 patients with just 13 cancer events total — far too few to draw firm conclusions about which treatment controls cancer better. The trial was not powered to prove non-inferiority or superiority for cancer control.

The numerical difference at 8 years (91% vs 84%) favors SBRT but was not statistically significant. At 5 years the rates were nearly identical (94% vs 95%). Longer follow-up and pooled analyses are needed.

Trial participants were treated at experienced centers; outcomes in other practice settings may differ (general medical guidance).

What To Watch

Longer PACE-A follow-up: Will the numerical SBRT advantage in cancer control persist or become statistically significant with more events?

Results from other PACE cohorts testing SBRT with hormone therapy for higher-risk localized disease.

Real-world outcome studies in community practice settings outside clinical trials (general medical guidance).
