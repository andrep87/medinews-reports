Major Screening Updates — New Blood and Stool Options, Wider Provider Access, and Evidence That Some Patients Can Wait Longer Between Follow-Up Colonoscopies

"National guidelines now endorse a blood test and two new at-home stool tests for average-risk adults starting at age 45, while Medicare expanded coverage to allow physician associates and advanced practice nurses to order these non-invasive screens. A large European trial found that waiting five years (instead of three) for the first surveillance colonoscopy after high-risk polyp removal was just as safe, potentially cutting procedures nearly in half."

The American Cancer Society (ACS) updated its 2026 screening guidelines to add three non-colonoscopy options for average-risk adults aged 45-75: an updated multitarget stool DNA test (Cologuard, every 3 years), a new multitarget stool RNA test (ColoSense, every 3 years), and — for patients who decline or cannot complete stool-based tests — a blood test called Shield (every 3 years). Shield has lower sensitivity for early-stage cancers and precancerous polyps than stool tests. Colonoscopy remains the gold standard.
The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) changed its National Coverage Determination so that physician associates (PAs) and advanced practice registered nurses (APRNs) can now order all Medicare-covered non-invasive screening tests — including stool DNA, stool RNA, blood-based, and fecal occult blood tests — directly.
The European Polyp Surveillance (EPoS) II trial randomized 10,799 patients with completely removed high-risk adenomas to a first surveillance colonoscopy at 3 years vs 5 years. At 5.5 years, colorectal cancer incidence was 0.77% in the 5-year group vs 0.82% in the 3-year group — a difference that met the pre-specified non-inferiority margin. Cancer stage at detection and mortality were similar.
Mississippi launched a statewide "MS80 by 2030" campaign targeting ages 45–59, where only 38% of adults 45–49 and 62% of those 50–59 are up to date on screening, aiming to reach 80% participation.
More choices mean more people get screened: over 20 million eligible Americans are not up to date. The blood test (Shield) is specifically recommended for patients who decline or cannot complete stool tests, though it has lower sensitivity for early-stage cancers and precancerous polyps.
Allowing PAs and APRNs to order tests removes a scheduling bottleneck — patients no longer need a separate physician visit just to get a screening order, speeding access especially in rural or underserved areas.
The EPoS II data challenge a decades-old habit of 3-year surveillance after high-risk polyps. Nearly 4,200 fewer colonoscopies were performed in the 5-year arm with no increase in cancer risk, meaning less burden, lower cost, and fewer procedure complications for patients.
Any positive non-colonoscopy test (blood or stool) still requires a follow-up colonoscopy, ideally within 6 months, to confirm and remove any precancerous lesions.
Average-risk adults aged 45–75 who are due for screening — including those who have avoided colonoscopy due to prep, anesthesia, time off work, or access barriers.
Patients who have had high-risk adenomas removed (defined as ≥10 mm, high-grade dysplasia, villous features, or 3–10 adenomas) and are facing their first surveillance colonoscopy decision — the 5-year interval now has randomized trial support.
Medicare beneficiaries whose primary clinician is a PA or APRN — they can now order Shield, Cologuard, ColoSense, or FOBT directly.
Residents of states with low screening rates (like Mississippi) where targeted outreach and at-home options may close gaps.
Ask your clinician: "Am I eligible for the new blood test (Shield) or the updated stool tests (Cologuard, ColoSense) instead of colonoscopy?" — and confirm which is covered by your insurance.
If you had high-risk polyps removed, ask: "Based on the new EPoS II trial, can my first surveillance colonoscopy be at 5 years instead of 3?" — bring the NEJM reference (DOI: 10.1056/nejmoa2603816) to the discussion.
If your primary provider is a PA or APRN, confirm they are aware of the new CMS rule allowing them to order non-invasive screening tests directly — this can save you a separate doctor visit.
Schedule any positive stool or blood test follow-up colonoscopy within 6 months — do not delay, as the protective benefit depends on timely polyp removal.

The Hope

  • Hope: More people will get screened because they have acceptable alternatives to colonoscopy. Early detection yields >90% 5-year survival.
  • Reality: Blood and stool tests miss some precancerous polyps and early cancers. A negative result is not a "clean bill of health" — it means "no signal detected this time." Colonoscopy remains the only test that prevents cancer by removing polyps.

The Reality

  • The 5-year surveillance interval is not yet standard of care in the U.S. Guidelines still say 3 years. Discuss with your gastroenterologist before changing your schedule.
  • Insurance coverage for new tests varies; prior authorization delays are common. Medicare covers Shield, but some private plans may not yet cover ColoSense.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Shield (Guardant Health): Blood-based cell-free DNA test; recommended every 3 years for average-risk adults who decline or cannot complete stool tests. Has lower sensitivity for advanced adenomas and early-stage cancers compared to stool tests and colonoscopy.

Cologuard (Exact Sciences): Updated multitarget stool DNA test; every 3 years; detects DNA markers and hemoglobin (general medical guidance).

ColoSense (Geneoscopy): Multitarget stool RNA test; newly added to ACS guidelines; every 3 years (general medical guidance).

Fecal immunochemical test (FIT) / FOBT [fecal occult blood test]: Remain recommended annual options; now orderable by PAs/APRNs under Medicare.

Evidence Uncertainty

ACS guideline update incorporates newly available test performance data — but no new randomized trial outcomes comparing Shield, updated Cologuard, or ColoSense against colonoscopy.

EPoS II is an interim analysis at 5.5 years; the primary endpoint is 10 years. Long-term safety of 5-year intervals remains unproven. The trial was European; U.S. population and polyp detection rates may differ.

Shield's sensitivity for advanced adenomas is lower than stool DNA tests and much lower than colonoscopy — a negative Shield result does not rule out precancerous lesions as reliably. However, Shield offers a non-invasive option for patients who would otherwise not be screened at all.

CMS rule change is administrative — it expands who can order tests, not which tests are covered. Medicare coverage for the new stool RNA test (ColoSense) is still being determined (general medical guidance).

What To Watch

EPoS II 10-year results (primary endpoint) — will confirm or refute the safety of 5-year surveillance intervals.

USPSTF guideline update — their endorsement drives no-cost coverage under the Affordable Care Act; 60+ cancer groups are urging protection of the Task Force's independence.

CMS coverage decision for ColoSense (stool RNA test) and any future blood-based tests.

Real-world adoption data: how many patients choose blood vs stool vs colonoscopy, and whether overall screening rates rise.
