Once-Weekly Basal Insulin Approvals Offer Major Injection Burden Reduction for Type 2 Diabetes

"The FDA approved Onswik (insulin efsitora alfa-gobe, Eli Lilly) on September 24, 2026, as a once-weekly basal insulin [a long-acting insulin that provides background blood sugar control] for adults with type 2 diabetes. This is the second once-weekly insulin available in the US, following Awiqli (insulin icodec, Novo Nordisk), approved in March 2026. Both reduce annual injections from about 365 to just 52, potentially overcoming a major barrier to starting and staying on insulin."

The FDA approved Onswik (insulin efsitora alfa-gobe) on September 24, 2026, as a once-weekly basal insulin [a long-acting insulin that maintains background blood sugar control] for adults with type 2 diabetes. This is the fourth global approval after the EU, Mexico, and Japan.
Approval is based on the QWINT Phase 3 program involving over 3,400 adults across four trials comparing once-weekly efsitora to daily insulin glargine or insulin degludec [two commonly used daily basal insulins].
Novo Nordisk's Awiqli (insulin icodec) received FDA approval in March 2026 as the first once-weekly basal insulin in the US (general medical guidance).
Efsitora is a fusion protein [a molecule combining insulin with a long-lasting protein backbone] with a half-life of about 17 days, allowing weekly dosing. It is available in two concentrated pen formulations: U-500 and U-1000 KwikPen.
Once-weekly dosing reduces annual injections from about 365 to 52, potentially overcoming 'injection fatigue' — a major reason patients and doctors delay starting insulin.
In the QWINT-1 trial, efsitora required fewer dose adjustments over 52 weeks compared to daily glargine, suggesting simpler dose management.
Efsitora showed a 43% lower rate of clinically significant or severe hypoglycemia [dangerous low blood sugar] compared to glargine in patients new to insulin (rate ratio 0.57). However, in other QWINT trials comparing efsitora to degludec, hypoglycemia rates were not significantly different.
Having two competing weekly insulins from different manufacturers may improve access and affordability over time (general medical guidance).
Adults with type 2 diabetes who need to start basal insulin or who are considering switching from daily basal insulin.
Patients who find daily injections burdensome — due to needle anxiety, busy schedules, dexterity issues, or difficulty remembering daily doses (general medical guidance).
Not for type 1 diabetes — efsitora carries increased severe hypoglycemia risk in type 1 diabetes (QWINT-5 showed 10% severe hypoglycemia vs. 3% with degludec). Awiqli's type 1 diabetes indication was also rejected by the FDA.
Available in concentrated pens (U-500 and U-1000 KwikPen) — these deliver higher doses per injection than standard U-100 insulin, so careful dose education is essential to avoid errors.
Ask your diabetes care team: 'Am I a candidate for once-weekly basal insulin? How would switching from my current daily insulin work?'
If starting insulin for the first time, discuss whether once-weekly dosing fits your lifestyle and ability to keep a fixed weekly schedule.
Request hands-on training with the specific pen device (KwikPen for Onswik) before first use — the concentrated formulations (U-500/U-1000) have different dosing increments than standard insulin pens, and mix-ups can cause dangerous dosing errors.
Confirm insurance coverage and out-of-pocket cost; ask about manufacturer savings programs if cost is a barrier.

The Hope

  • Hope: Dramatically reduced injection burden may improve adherence, reduce delays in starting insulin, and help more patients reach blood sugar targets earlier.
  • Reality: Weekly dosing requires strict schedule adherence — missing a dose has longer consequences than missing a daily dose. Concentrated pen formulations (U-500/U-1000) increase the risk of dosing errors without thorough education.

The Reality

  • Cost and access: Onswik pricing not yet public at launch; Awiqli list price approximately $99/package with access programs bringing it to $35-55/month. Insurance coverage and formulary placement will determine real-world accessibility.
  • Not a cure: Still requires blood sugar monitoring, dose adjustments, and lifestyle management. Hypoglycemia risk remains, especially with concentrated formulations.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Onswik (insulin efsitora alfa-gobe, Eli Lilly): Once-weekly Fc-fusion basal insulin [a long-acting insulin attached to a protein backbone for slow release], FDA approved September 2026 for type 2 diabetes; available in U-500 and U-1000 KwikPen.

Awiqli (insulin icodec, Novo Nordisk): Once-weekly basal insulin, FDA approved March 2026 for type 2 diabetes (general medical guidance).

Both insulins are approved for type 2 diabetes only. Neither is approved for type 1 diabetes due to increased severe hypoglycemia risk.

Additional GLP-1 receptor agonists [drugs that mimic a gut hormone to lower blood sugar and promote weight loss] and dual/triple agonists are in late-stage development, potentially offering further options for type 2 diabetes management (general medical guidance).

Evidence Uncertainty

QWINT trials (n>3,400): Onswik met the primary endpoint of non-inferior HbA1c [average blood sugar over 3 months] reduction vs. daily basal insulins across insulin-naive and insulin-experienced patients.

Key uncertainty: Long-term cardiovascular outcomes, kidney safety, and real-world effectiveness in diverse populations are not yet established; post-marketing studies are required by the FDA.

QWINT-1 showed 43% lower hypoglycemia rate with efsitora vs. glargine (rate ratio 0.57), but other QWINT trials comparing to degludec showed no significant difference — the hypoglycemia advantage may depend on the comparator.

Weekly dosing means dose adjustments take longer to reach steady state [stable blood levels]. Careful transition planning with your care team is essential when switching from daily insulin (general medical guidance).

What To Watch

Onswik US launch 'in coming months' (per Lilly); monitor real-world adoption, dosing error reports, and how it compares to Awiqli in practice.

Long-term safety data for both weekly insulins; the FDA may require dedicated cardiovascular outcomes trials.

Whether insurance formularies favor one weekly insulin over the other, affecting patient access and cost.
