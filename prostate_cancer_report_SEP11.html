Active Surveillance Becomes the Standard for Low-Risk Prostate Cancer, But Many Men Still Miss Out

"A landmark VA study shows 93% of veterans with low-risk prostate cancer now choose active surveillance — monitoring with regular tests instead of immediate surgery or radiation — up from just 27% two decades ago. This approach delivers the same survival while avoiding incontinence, erectile dysfunction, and bowel problems. However, outside the VA system, fewer than 60% of eligible U.S. men receive this option, and Black and Hispanic patients are less likely to be offered it regardless of cancer features."

Researchers tracked over 73,000 veterans diagnosed with low-risk or favorable intermediate-risk prostate cancer across the VA system from 2005 to 2024.
In 2024, 93% of veterans with low-risk disease chose active surveillance — regular PSA [prostate-specific antigen] blood tests, MRI scans, and repeat biopsies [tissue samples] — instead of immediate treatment, up from 27% in 2005.
For favorable intermediate-risk disease, active surveillance use rose from 14% to 61% over the same period.
The VA's integrated system — shared records, quality tracking, and long-term doctor-patient continuity — drove adoption far beyond the national average, where under 60% of low-risk men receive surveillance.
Low-risk prostate cancer (Gleason Grade Group 1 [a measure of how aggressive cancer cells look under a microscope]) almost never spreads. Treating it immediately exposes men to lifelong side effects without a survival benefit.
Active surveillance avoids urinary incontinence [leaking urine], erectile dysfunction [trouble getting or keeping an erection], and bowel issues from surgery or radiation in men whose cancer would never threaten their life.
The VA data strongly suggest that a system-wide commitment to guideline-based care can nearly eliminate overtreatment — but most U.S. men are treated in fragmented systems where surveillance rates lag.
Men diagnosed with low-risk prostate cancer: PSA under 10 ng/mL, Gleason Grade Group 1 (3+3=6), and cancer confined to a small portion of the prostate.
Men with favorable intermediate-risk disease: Grade Group 2 (3+4=7) in a limited number of biopsy cores, PSA under 10 ng/mL — surveillance rose to 55–88% depending on specific features.
Black and Hispanic men, and those in economically disadvantaged areas, were less likely to be offered surveillance even with identical cancer features — this disparity needs active attention.
If your PSA is elevated, ask your doctor: 'Should I get an MRI and biomarker tests (like the 4Kscore, PHI, or new PSB blood test) before deciding on a biopsy?'
If diagnosed with Grade Group 1 cancer, ask: 'Am I a candidate for active surveillance? What is the monitoring schedule — PSA every 3–6 months, MRI at 12 months, repeat biopsy at 12–18 months?'
Request a clear explanation of your Gleason Grade Group and whether your cancer meets criteria for surveillance (Grade Group 1, or favorable Grade Group 2 with low volume).
If you are Black, have a family history, or carry a BRCA gene mutation, start screening discussions at age 40–45 per guidelines — do not wait until 50.

The Hope

  • Hope: Most men with low-risk disease can safely avoid treatment side effects for decades; many never need intervention.
  • Reality: Surveillance requires discipline — missed appointments or biopsies risk missing progression. Anxiety about 'untreated cancer' is real and leads some to request treatment anyway.

The Reality

  • Reality: Outside VA systems, you may need to advocate for surveillance; some urology practices still treat all low-risk patients immediately.
  • Reality: Disparities persist — Black men are about 70% more likely to be diagnosed and twice as likely to die from prostate cancer, partly due to later-stage diagnosis and unequal access to guideline-concordant care.

PSMA PET/CT [a specialized scan that detects prostate cancer spread] (Pylarify, Illuccix): now standard for staging; detects spread earlier than CT or bone scan, especially when PSA is low.

Triggers for treatment on surveillance: grade upgrade to Group 2 or higher, significant volume increase on MRI/biopsy, or PSA doubling time under 3 years (general medical guidance).

Surveillance disparities: Black/Hispanic veterans and those in high-disadvantage areas had lower surveillance odds independent of clinical factors — cause unclear (access, communication, bias?).

The long-term psychological burden of 'living with cancer' is unmeasured; some men still choose treatment due to anxiety despite counseling.

Treatment Holidays After Deep Response to Hormone Therapy Are Feasible and Preserve Quality of Life

"For men with metastatic prostate cancer who achieve a deep, sustained response to modern hormone therapy (ADT [androgen deprivation therapy] plus an ARPI [androgen receptor pathway inhibitor]), a planned treatment holiday appears safe and effective in a small retrospective study. Median treatment-free survival was 35 months — longer than the 26 months patients spent on treatment — and one-third remained off therapy at five years. Restarting the same drugs after progression worked in 96% of cases, without triggering worse resistance. These findings are hypothesis-generating and need prospective trial validation."

Researchers analyzed 69 men with metastatic hormone-sensitive (mHSPC) or castration-resistant (mCRPC) prostate cancer who had received ADT [hormone therapy that lowers testosterone] plus an ARPI [a drug that blocks the androgen receptor, such as enzalutamide, abiraterone, or darolutamide] for at least 12 months with no progression and a deep response (very low PSA plus PSMA PET/CT [a specialized scan] showing no visible disease).
After stopping treatment, median treatment-free survival was 35 months; 56% were treatment-free at 2 years, 34% at 5 years. Note: this 35-month figure may reflect a specific subgroup (patients with prior prostate radiotherapy) rather than the overall cohort — the published breakdown should be consulted for details.
Of 33 men who progressed, 27 restarted the same ADT + ARPI combination; 96% responded again.
Men who were initially hormone-sensitive (mHSPC) had longer holidays; castration-resistant (mCRPC) patients had more than double the progression risk after stopping.
No evidence that stopping and restarting triggered worse drug resistance — disease control was re-established in nearly all who were rechallenged.
ADT + ARPI causes cumulative side effects: fatigue, hot flashes, muscle loss, bone thinning, sexual dysfunction, metabolic changes, and cardiovascular risk. A holiday of 2–5 years dramatically reduces this burden.
Financial burden also drops: oral ARPIs can cost thousands of dollars per month, and ADT injections require clinic visits. Stopping saves money and time (general medical guidance).
The 'deep responder' profile (very low PSA plus no visible disease on PSMA PET/CT) identifies who may safely pause — this is a biomarker-driven de-escalation strategy.
The fact that restarting the same drugs worked in 96% means the holiday is a true pause, not a dead end — the same effective regimen remains available.
Men with metastatic prostate cancer (hormone-sensitive or castration-resistant) who have been on ADT + ARPI for at least 12 months without progression.
Men with a deep response: PSA undetectable or very low, and PSMA PET/CT showing no visible disease (complete metabolic response).
Particularly applicable to men who were initially hormone-sensitive (mHSPC) and achieved a deep response.
Not for men with incomplete responses, high-volume disease in organs (visceral metastases), or rapidly rising PSA on therapy (general medical guidance).
If you have been on ADT + ARPI for over a year with excellent control (PSA <0.2 ng/mL, clear PSMA PET/CT), ask your oncologist: 'Am I a candidate for a planned treatment holiday? What are the monitoring rules?'
Request a PSMA PET/CT scan to document depth of response before discussing a holiday — this imaging is now widely available and often covered by insurance (general medical guidance).
Agree on a specific restart trigger with your doctor: PSA rise above a set threshold (such as >0.2 or >2 ng/mL), new lesions on imaging, or symptomatic progression (general medical guidance).
Clarify with your doctor exactly what stops during the holiday — this study stopped both ADT and ARPI, but some protocols may continue ADT injections. The approach varies (general medical guidance).
Track symptoms monthly (fatigue, bone pain, urinary changes) and keep scheduled PSA checks every 3 months. Do not skip monitoring.

  • Reality: Not every deep responder qualifies — requires multidisciplinary discussion, reliable follow-up, and patient commitment to monitoring.
  • Reality: Progression during a holiday can be psychologically hard; some men prefer staying on treatment for the sense of control. Restarting after a break can feel like failure (general medical guidance).

  • Reality: This is not a cure — metastatic prostate cancer remains incurable. The holiday delays but does not eliminate the need for lifelong therapy.

Limitation: Heterogeneous ARPI types, treatment durations, and definitions of 'deep response' (PSMA PET criteria not uniform).

Unknown: Optimal monitoring frequency during holiday; whether intermittent ADT (vs. continuous) is safe; long-term survival impact vs. continuous therapy.

Structured Exercise and Lifestyle Program (STAMINA) Cuts ADT Side Effects by One-Third at Low Cost

"The STAMINA trial — the largest of its kind — shows that a 12-month supervised exercise, diet, and behavioral support program delivered in community gyms reduces quality-of-life decline by up to one-third in men on androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) [hormone therapy for prostate cancer]. The program costs £13,920–£19,077 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY), well below the NHS cost-effectiveness threshold of £30,000, making it one of the most cost-effective supportive care interventions in oncology. Over 80% adherence shows men will stick with it when it's prescribed as part of routine care."

700 men on ADT (median age 71.6, 34% metastatic, 19% also on ARPI or chemotherapy) were randomized 5:4 to the STAMINA program vs. enhanced usual care (clinician training plus behavioral prompts).
STAMINA: 12 months of supervised aerobic plus resistance exercise in community gyms (Nuffield Health), dietary advice, behavioral support, and free gym membership.
Primary outcome: FACT-P [a prostate cancer-specific quality-of-life questionnaire] improved by 4.5 points (p=0.0004); FACT-G [general quality of life] improved by 3.7 points — both exceed the 3-point threshold considered clinically meaningful.
Fatigue (FACIT-F [a fatigue questionnaire]) improved by 1.9 points (p=0.0068); adherence was over 80% at 12 months.
Cost-effectiveness: £13,920–£19,077 per QALY [quality-adjusted life year, a measure of how much health a treatment buys] vs. the £30,000 NICE threshold; high probability of cost-effectiveness for up to 10 years.
ADT causes fatigue, weight gain around the waist, muscle loss, bone thinning, cardiovascular risk, and psychological distress — affecting over 300,000 UK men and roughly 500,000+ U.S. men on ADT at any given time.
STAMINA is the first lifestyle intervention integrated into routine cancer care at scale across a national health system — designed for real-world delivery, not just a research setting.
Benefits were sustained across physical, functional, emotional, and social well-being for the full 12 months.
Cost per QALY is about half that of most cancer drugs — this is high-value care that improves daily living, not just survival.
Any man starting or continuing ADT for prostate cancer, regardless of stage — the trial included localized, biochemical recurrence, and metastatic patients.
Men experiencing ADT side effects: fatigue, weight gain, muscle weakness, low mood, hot flashes, sexual dysfunction.
Men with cardiovascular risk factors (high blood pressure, diabetes, high cholesterol) — ADT worsens these; exercise directly counters them (general medical guidance).
Older men (median age 71 in trial) — the program was safe and effective in this age group. Only 3 intervention-related serious adverse events occurred, all resolved.
Ask your oncologist or urologist: 'Can you refer me to a supervised exercise program for men on ADT? Is there a STAMINA-style program or cancer rehabilitation service at this hospital or in the community?'
Request a baseline assessment: DEXA scan [bone density test], body composition, lipid panel [cholesterol], HbA1c [blood sugar average], blood pressure — track these every 6–12 months on ADT (general medical guidance).
Start now if no supervised program is available: aim for 150 minutes per week of moderate aerobic activity (brisk walking, cycling) plus 2 resistance sessions per week (bands, weights, bodyweight exercises). Use the 'talk test' — you should be breathing harder but able to speak (general medical guidance).
Ask for a cardiology or cardio-oncology referral if you have existing heart disease or multiple risk factors — ESC data show routine cardiology referral lowers cholesterol and improves outcomes in prostate cancer patients.

Genomic Testing Now Directs Life-Extending Therapy Earlier — Ask for It at Diagnosis

One combination (olaparib plus abiraterone, PROpel trial) extended median survival by about 7.4 months; another (talazoparib plus enzalutamide, TALAPRO-2 trial) cut the risk of progression or death by 55% in HRR-mutated patients. Meanwhile, Pluvicto (PSMA-targeted radiation) and Nubeqa (darolutamide) have moved from last-resort to first-line metastatic use. Genomic testing is no longer optional; it determines which of these life-extending options you qualify for."
