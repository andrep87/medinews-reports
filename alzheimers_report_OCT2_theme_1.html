Anti-Amyloid Therapies Enter Real-World Use—Access, Diagnosis, and Monitoring Challenges Emerge

"Two FDA-approved anti-amyloid drugs (Leqembi and Kisunla) now modestly slow early Alzheimer’s, but most patients face barriers: restrictive eligibility criteria exclude atypical presentations, commercial insurers often deny coverage, and required MRI monitoring for brain swelling (ARIA) strains clinic capacity. Blood tests like p-tau217 are starting to replace invasive spinal taps for diagnosis, yet equity gaps persist—women, Black, and Hispanic patients are less likely to receive treatment."

Two FDA-approved anti-amyloid antibodies — Leqembi (lecanemab) and Kisunla (donanemab) — are now in real-world use. Both clear amyloid plaques [protein deposits in the Alzheimer's brain] and modestly slow decline in early symptomatic disease.
Access is the dominant barrier. Commercial insurers frequently deny or delay coverage even when patients meet clinical criteria.
A published analysis found that patients with atypical Alzheimer's [forms that affect vision, language, or planning before memory] are frequently excluded by trial-based eligibility criteria, despite having confirmed Alzheimer's pathology.
Required MRI monitoring for ARIA [amyloid-related imaging abnormalities — brain swelling or small bleeds] places real strain on radiology capacity as treatment volumes grow.
Blood tests such as p-tau217 are beginning to replace spinal taps as a first diagnostic step, though equity gaps in access persist.
These are the first treatments that act on Alzheimer's underlying biology rather than only easing symptoms — but the benefit depends on being diagnosed early and actually getting access.
Eligibility criteria built around memory-predominant presentations systematically exclude people with atypical Alzheimer's, who are often younger and have the same underlying disease (general medical guidance).
ARIA monitoring is not optional. The infrastructure required — scanner time, radiologist reading capacity, protocols for acting on findings — is a genuine constraint on how widely these drugs can be delivered.
Blood-based diagnosis could widen access by reducing reliance on PET scanners and lumbar punctures, which are concentrated in specialty centers (general medical guidance).
Adults with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to Alzheimer's, with amyloid pathology confirmed by an accepted method.

If you have commercial insurance and are denied, ask your clinician's office about a peer-to-peer appeal and whether a case manager can be assigned (general medical guidance).
Ask what the MRI monitoring schedule will be for your treatment and what symptoms should prompt you to call between scans — ARIA can be silent, which is why the imaging schedule matters (general medical guidance).
If you have atypical symptoms, seek a dementia specialist who can advocate for access, since standard cognitive thresholds may exclude you despite early-stage disease.

The Hope

  • Hope: The first treatments that alter the disease process, with blood tests beginning to make diagnosis faster and less invasive.
  • Reality: The effect is modest — these drugs may delay worsening, not stop or reverse it. ARIA requires ongoing MRI surveillance that not every center can deliver (general medical guidance).

The Reality

  • Reality: Coverage denials, specialist shortages, and infusion and imaging capacity all limit who actually receives treatment (general medical guidance).
  • Reality: Clearing amyloid does not restore lost neurons. Tau pathology and neurodegeneration continue (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Leqembi (lecanemab) and Kisunla (donanemab): FDA-approved anti-amyloid antibodies for early symptomatic Alzheimer's. Both require ARIA monitoring by MRI.

Blood-based biomarkers including p-tau217 are being incorporated into diagnostic pathways as a first-line step before confirmatory testing.

Radiology capacity and care-pathway design are now recognized as rate-limiting factors in delivering these therapies at scale.

A prevention trial of trontinemab, another anti-amyloid antibody, is referenced in the sources, though trial details were not provided.

Evidence Uncertainty

Both drugs show statistically significant slowing of decline, but the absolute difference on cognitive rating scales is small, and whether it is clinically meaningful to patients and families is debated (general medical guidance).

ARIA is the principal safety concern. Risk is higher in people carrying two copies of the APOE4 gene variant (general medical guidance).

The atypical-presentation eligibility analysis documents exclusion but does not establish whether these patients would benefit similarly if treated.

Long-term questions remain open: how long treatment should continue, whether benefit persists beyond trial periods, and how these drugs perform in populations underrepresented in the trials.
