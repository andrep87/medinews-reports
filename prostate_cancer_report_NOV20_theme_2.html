DASL-HiCaP Trial Shows Darolutamide + Radiation/ADT Significantly Delays Metastases in Very High-Risk Localized Prostate Cancer

"The phase III [large-scale late-stage clinical trial testing effectiveness and safety] DASL-HiCaP trial found that adding darolutamide [an androgen receptor pathway inhibitor, a hormone-blocking pill] to 24 months of radiation and ADT [androgen deprivation therapy, hormone therapy that lowers male hormones] improved four-year metastasis-free survival [time without cancer spreading to distant sites] from 88% to 95% in very high-risk localized [confined to the prostate area] or recurrent [returned after prior treatment] prostate cancer[cite: 3, 4]. These early results point to a potential new treatment-intensification option with a side-effect profile close to placebo [an inactive look-alike treatment][cite: 3, 4]."

DASL-HiCaP randomly assigned more than 1,100 men with very high-risk localized or recurrent prostate cancer to radiation plus 24 months of ADT with either darolutamide or placebo[cite: 3, 4].
In the localized setting, "very high-risk" meant Grade Group 5 [Gleason score 9–10, the highest tumor grade group], pelvic lymph node involvement on standard imaging, or Grade Group 4 [Gleason 8] plus at least one extra risk feature (clinical T2b or higher, scan-detected T3a or higher, or PSA above 20 ng/mL)[cite: 3, 4].
At a four-year median follow-up interim analysis [an early planned look at study data], adding darolutamide improved four-year metastasis-free survival on conventional imaging from 88% to 95% (hazard ratio [a measure of relative event risk over time] 0.42, 95% confidence interval [a statistical range of likely values] 0.27–0.66, P = 0.0001)[cite: 3, 4].
Benefits were seen across pre-specified subgroups—including men with pelvic lymph nodes and those receiving salvage [post-surgery] radiation—and 99% of men on darolutamide were free of castration-resistant prostate cancer [cancer that grows despite low testosterone levels] at four years (hazard ratio 0.22)[cite: 3, 4].
DASL-HiCaP adds to studies of hormone-blocking intensification alongside radiation and ADT—alongside STAMPEDE (with abiraterone) and ENZARAD (with enzalutamide, particularly in pelvic node-positive disease)—specifically testing darolutamide in a defined very high-risk group[cite: 3, 4].
In the trial report, darolutamide lowered metastasis risk by 58% with side effects—including heart and blood-vessel events—similar to placebo, whereas abiraterone requires daily prednisone [a steroid medication] and carries different metabolic and heart risks[cite: 3, 4] (independent fact-check: external coverage also notes higher overall grade 3 or higher adverse events with darolutamide vs. placebo [43% vs. 36%] while overall cardiovascular and fracture rates were similar)[cite: 3, 4].
The study required modern radiation doses (at least 78 Gy [grays, a unit of radiation dose] to the prostate in the primary setting, 64 Gy in salvage therapy, and pelvic nodal radiation given to over 90% of participants), supporting that the gain came from darolutamide rather than suboptimal radiation[cite: 3, 4].
Focusing intensification on higher-risk patients helps avoid adding extra drug treatment to lower-tier high-risk men who often do well with standard care[cite: 3, 4].
Men with newly diagnosed very high-risk localized prostate cancer—defined by Grade Group 5, pelvic lymph nodes seen on conventional CT [computed tomography], MRI, or bone scan, or Grade Group 4 with clinical T2b+, scan-detected T3a+, or PSA over 20 ng/mL[cite: 3, 4].
Men with PSA recurrence after surgery (PSA rising within 12 months to at least 0.1 ng/mL) who also have Grade Group 5, pelvic lymph node disease, or Grade Group 4 with T3a+[cite: 3, 4].
Patients receiving curative-intent radiation (primary or salvage) who can take 24 months of ADT (median age was 72 in the primary group and 65–66 in the salvage group)[cite: 3, 4].
Based on the trial population, this study focused on localized or post-surgery recurrent disease rather than distant metastatic disease or men unable to take ADT and radiation[cite: 3, 4].
If you have high-risk localized or recurrent prostate cancer, you may wish to ask your radiation oncologist: "Do I meet the DASL-HiCaP very high-risk criteria, and how do my staging scans and tumor grade fit?"[cite: 3, 4]
You may wish to ask how pelvic lymph nodes were evaluated—DASL-HiCaP used conventional imaging (CT, MRI, or bone scan) rather than PSMA PET [prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography, a specialized scan] alone to define nodal eligibility[cite: 3, 4].
You may wish to ask your care team whether adding darolutamide is being considered through a clinical trial or off-label discussion, given that regulatory approval for this specific setting is still pending[cite: 3, 4].
If you are starting radiation plus 24 months of ADT, you may wish to review whether pelvic lymph node radiation and modern radiation dosing are part of your plan[cite: 3, 4].

The Hope

  • Hope: Adding darolutamide lowered four-year metastasis rates by 6 percentage points (58% relative reduction) and delayed castration-resistant disease in a study where side effects were largely similar to placebo[cite: 3, 4].
  • Reality: The regimen is not yet FDA-approved for this setting, and insurance coverage or out-of-pocket medication costs can create practical hurdles[cite: 3, 4] (general medical guidance).

The Reality

  • Hope: Delaying spread of the cancer can help preserve quality of life for men going through long-term hormone and radiation treatment[cite: 3, 4].
  • Reality: These are interim findings rather than a guarantee of cure, and overall survival is still being followed[cite: 3, 4].

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Darolutamide is an oral hormone-blocking therapy being evaluated for this localized very high-risk setting and is not yet approved by the FDA for this specific use[cite: 3, 4] (independent fact-check: darolutamide is FDA-approved for non-metastatic castration-resistant and metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer, though those approvals were not listed in this week's articles)[cite: 3, 4].

Abiraterone plus ADT and radiation has evidence from STAMPEDE but involves daily prednisone and distinct side-effect risks such as high blood pressure, liver effects, and heart issues[cite: 3, 4].

Enzalutamide was studied in ENZARAD with benefit seen in men with pelvic nodal disease[cite: 3, 4].

Investigators noted an ongoing study testing darolutamide with radiation in a broader high-risk setting, alongside ongoing research into intensified regimens such as PEACE-1[cite: 3, 4].

Evidence Uncertainty

DASL-HiCaP is a large randomized phase III trial (over 1,100 patients) with contemporary radiation and high treatment completion (about 80% completed therapy, with under 1% never starting), but these results come from a pre-planned interim analysis[cite: 3, 4].

Overall survival and prostate cancer-specific survival data are still maturing—only 11 prostate cancer deaths (7 on placebo, 4 on darolutamide) and 45 total deaths had occurred at this early look[cite: 3, 4].

Risk profiles differ across trials (for example, DASL-HiCaP had 71–74% Grade Group 5 and 29% pelvic node involvement, placing it higher risk than ENZARAD and ATLAS but lower risk than STAMPEDE), so cross-trial comparisons require caution[cite: 3, 4].

Longer follow-up is needed to confirm late side effects and whether molecular markers such as HRR [homologous recombination repair, a DNA-repair pathway] status or Decipher scores identify specific responders[cite: 3, 4].

What To Watch

Later follow-up and final overall survival results from DASL-HiCaP as survival events mature[cite: 3, 4].

Regulatory review steps and potential guideline updates regarding darolutamide in very high-risk localized or recurrent prostate cancer[cite: 3, 4].

Results from ongoing studies of darolutamide with radiation in broader high-risk groups[cite: 3, 4].

Biomarker analyses (such as HRR status, Decipher, and genomic subprotocols) looking at who benefits most from adding extra therapy[cite: 3, 4].
