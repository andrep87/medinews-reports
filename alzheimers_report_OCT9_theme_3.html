Earlier Treatment May Deliver Greater Value — and Policy and Access Are Catching Up

"New economic modeling estimates that the Alzheimer's drug donanemab (Kisunla) generates about $104,900 in lifetime societal value per patient, rising 32% to about $138,300 if treatment starts two years earlier. Several US states now require insurance coverage for these treatments and for biomarker testing, while new at-home (subcutaneous) dosing and telehealth models aim to move care out of infusion centers. These value figures come from a model, not from measured outcomes."

USC Schaeffer Center modeling estimated that donanemab (Kisunla, an amyloid-clearing drug) adds about 0.37 years free of disability and 0.63 years of community (non-nursing-home) living per patient, for about $104,900 in lifetime societal value. Starting two years earlier raised that by 32% (to about $138,300), and a stronger early-effect scenario raised it by 71% (to about $179,400).
California (SB 950) requires state-regulated plans to cover FDA-approved disease-modifying treatments without step therapy [being required to try cheaper drugs first]. It took effect Jan 1, 2025.
Delaware (SB 120) requires Medicaid and private insurance to cover biomarker testing (blood, spinal fluid, or PET) when medically indicated, applying to policies issued or renewed after Dec 31, 2027.
Illinois (SB 2886) extends genetic-privacy protections to biomarker test results, barring employers and insurers from using them for hiring, firing, or underwriting, effective Jan 1, 2027.
A lecanemab at-home autoinjector (Leqembi Iqlik) received FDA approval for weekly maintenance dosing in 2025 and for treatment initiation in 2026 — described as the first disease-modifying therapy that can be given from the start without infusion-center visits.
The Alzheimer's Association launched a Brain Health & Quality Care Initiative (BHQCI) with Eisai and Eli Lilly to standardize cognitive assessment, diagnosis, and referral pathways in primary care.
In the UK, a £48M BARBARA Alliance was announced to speed trial recruitment and prepare the NHS for next-generation treatments.
On average, Alzheimer's is diagnosed about 3.5 years after symptoms begin — a missed window, since the modeling suggests earlier treatment yields greater value, and US dementia costs were cited at about $818B in 2026.
The modeling shows earlier treatment produces more societal value; this is consistent with the general principle that neurodegenerative damage is hard to reverse, though that neurobiological point goes beyond what the economic model itself measured.
Banning step therapy (California) means patients no longer have to try older drugs such as cholinesterase inhibitors (for example, donepezil) before accessing disease-modifying treatment.
Pairing biomarker-coverage mandates (Delaware) with privacy protections (Illinois) means people can pursue diagnosis with less fear of job loss or insurance discrimination.
Combining at-home dosing, telehealth cognitive testing, at-home blood draws, and remote monitoring points toward a 'care-at-home' model that the sources describe as especially valuable for rural and underserved patients.
Adults with early memory concerns or mild cognitive impairment (MCI): this is the group where, according to the modeling, earlier detection matters most.
Patients in California, Delaware, or Illinois: state laws now support coverage for treatment and biomarker testing, which can be cited if a claim is denied.
Rural and underserved patients: the at-home model (subcutaneous lecanemab, telehealth, mobile blood draws, remote monitoring) described in the sources is aimed at improving your access.
Primary care teams: the BHQCI initiative offers a structured pathway from cognitive screening to blood biomarker to specialist referral and treatment.
People weighing anti-amyloid therapy: these drugs carry a risk of ARIA [amyloid-related imaging abnormalities — brain swelling or small bleeds], so benefits and risks should be discussed with a specialist (general medical guidance).
You may wish to ask your primary care doctor about a cognitive screening rather than waiting for symptoms to worsen; digital cognitive assessments are among the tools now available.
If a screening is abnormal, you may wish to ask whether a p-tau217 blood test could help gauge the likelihood of Alzheimer's before a specialist referral, which may shorten the path to answers.
If you are diagnosed with MCI or early Alzheimer's, you may wish to ask your neurologist whether you are eligible for lecanemab or donanemab, and whether the at-home (subcutaneous) option is appropriate.
If insurance denies coverage, you may wish to cite your state law (CA SB 950, DE SB 120, or IL SB 2886) and ask about an external review; patient-advocacy organizations can assist with appeals (general medical guidance).
You may wish to ask about joining a trial-readiness registry, such as those being connected by the BARBARA Alliance, to be matched to studies more quickly.
You may wish to discuss advance care planning while thinking and memory are intact, since earlier diagnosis gives more time to express your preferences (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • Hope: a pathway of diagnosis in primary care, a blood test, at-home treatment, and preserved independence — with real policy momentum from state laws, BHQCI, and BARBARA.
  • Reality: US dementia costs were about $818B in 2026, and the sources note long MRI and PET waitlists and gaps in primary-care readiness, though specific waitlist and workforce figures were not provided and have been removed.
  • Cost to patients: the sources do not give drug prices or out-of-pocket figures, so earlier specific dollar amounts have been removed; coverage and cost-sharing vary by plan (general medical guidance).

The Reality

  • Equity: at-home care helps rural access only where broadband, mobile blood draws, and scanning exist, and some patients still face systemic barriers; the sources note the value of community health workers for isolated patients (inference).
  • Not a cure: the modeling points to roughly one combined year of disability-free and community living gained per patient, with large societal value, but scaling this across everyone affected remains the central challenge.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Lecanemab (Leqembi): given by IV every two weeks, now also as a weekly at-home autoinjector (Leqembi Iqlik) — described as the first at-home disease-modifying therapy.

Donanemab (Kisunla): given by IV every four weeks after a loading period; an under-the-skin version (remternetug) is in Phase 3 testing.

Oral drugs in late-stage testing (reported as not associated with ARIA, and so without routine MRI monitoring): AR1001/mirodenafil (AriBio, Phase 3), buntanetap (Annovis, Phase 3), and Cobenfy/xanomeline-trospium (BMS, Phase 3 for psychosis) — all once-daily pills.

Diagnostic tools: the FDA-cleared Fujirebio p-tau217 blood test, digital cognitive assessments (Linus Health, Cogstate), and SIMOA plasma panels (Quanterix).

Access infrastructure: the Alzheimer's Association's BHQCI (primary-care pathways) and the UK's BARBARA Alliance (trial matching).

Evidence Uncertainty

Modeling strength: a dynamic microsimulation (USC Schaeffer Center) using national surveys, a trial effect size of about 29% slowing, and Medicaid claims; it is peer-reviewed in Alzheimer's & Dementia and uses conservative assumptions.

Durability is a key uncertainty: if the treatment effect lasts only about four years rather than a lifetime, the estimated value roughly halves (to about $49,100), though starting earlier still adds about 15%.

Real-world benefit may be smaller: the modeled group resembled selected Phase 3 trial participants (amyloid-positive, MCI or mild dementia, few other illnesses), so results may not transfer fully to the general population (inference).

Coverage gaps: state laws generally apply to state-regulated plans; the sources do not detail how self-funded or Medicare Advantage plans are affected, so those specifics are contextual rather than sourced.

At-home dosing still involves monitoring: subcutaneous anti-amyloid antibodies are expected to carry ARIA risk as a class effect and likely still require MRI monitoring, which limits truly 'home-only' care (inference).

What To Watch

Phase 3 results for subcutaneous donanemab (remternetug) — a second at-home option could increase competition.

Phase 3 results for oral drugs (AR1001, buntanetap, Cobenfy); if approved, pills could reduce reliance on infusions and routine MRI monitoring.

How widely health systems adopt the BHQCI pathway and whether it shortens the time from first concern to treatment.

Rollout of the UK BARBARA Alliance and how quickly it accelerates trial recruitment.

Whether blood-based diagnostics expand globally, including the Quanterix approvals in China (general medical guidance).
