New Evidence on Modifiable Dementia Risks — Hearing, Vitamin D, Aspirin, Statins, and More

"Several studies clarify which modifiable factors may affect dementia risk. Hearing aids improved cognition in some people with mild cognitive impairment but have not been shown to prevent dementia. Higher vitamin D intake was associated with better cognitive scores in an observational analysis. Low-dose aspirin was linked to substantially lower dementia risk in one genetic subgroup — but increased serious bleeding in that same group by a comparable amount. Findings on statins, periodontitis, air pollution, and glucosamine are earlier-stage."

Hearing aids: In people with mild cognitive impairment, hearing aid use was associated with cognitive recovery in a minority of patients. A separate study found hearing aid intervention did not improve dementia-level impairment. Dementia prevention remains unproven.
Vitamin D: Higher-dose vitamin D supplement use was associated with better cognitive test scores among people at dementia risk with sleep problems. This was an observational analysis of self-reported supplement use within a cohort, not a randomized trial (audit correction).
Aspirin: A genetic analysis within the ASPREE trial found that among participants in the highest 20% for a platelet-count genetic score, 1% of those taking low-dose aspirin developed dementia versus 3.3% on placebo — roughly a 70% lower relative risk. In the same subgroup, major bleeding occurred in 4.4% on aspirin versus 2.1% on placebo (independent fact-check).
Statins: Statin use in people with type 2 diabetes was associated with lower dementia risk in the cited research.
Other factors examined include anticoagulants, periodontitis [gum disease], air pollution, and glucosamine supplements, all at earlier evidence stages.
Modifiable risk factors are where individuals have the most direct influence, which makes the quality of evidence behind each one worth understanding (general medical guidance).
The aspirin finding illustrates why relative risk alone can mislead. The absolute dementia reduction in that subgroup was about 2.3 percentage points — and the absolute increase in major bleeding was also about 2.3 percentage points. The benefit and harm were of comparable size (independent fact-check).
Hearing and cognition are linked, but improving cognition in people who already have mild impairment is a different claim from preventing dementia in the first place.
Observational findings — including the vitamin D result — show associations, not that the supplement caused the difference. People who take supplements differ from those who don't in ways that are hard to fully adjust for (general medical guidance).
Older adults with hearing loss, particularly those with mild cognitive impairment.
People at elevated dementia risk who also have sleep difficulties, relevant to the vitamin D observation.
The aspirin finding applies only to a specific genetic subgroup defined by platelet-count genetics — it does not apply to the general population, and the genetic score used is not a routinely available clinical test.
People with type 2 diabetes, relevant to the statin finding.
Anyone currently taking aspirin or supplements, who should not change anything based on a newsletter (general medical guidance).
If you have hearing loss, get it assessed and treated. Hearing aids improve hearing and communication regardless of the dementia question, and that is reason enough (general medical guidance).
Do not start aspirin for dementia prevention based on this finding. The benefit was confined to one genetic subgroup, the genetic score is not a standard clinical test, and in that same subgroup major bleeding roughly doubled. Aspirin decisions should be made with your clinician based on your overall cardiovascular and bleeding risk (independent fact-check).
Do not stop glucosamine based on these findings. The sources explicitly state the evidence does not establish that people should discontinue it (audit correction).
If you are considering vitamin D supplementation, discuss it with your clinician rather than acting on an observational association (general medical guidance).
If you have type 2 diabetes, statin decisions should follow your overall cardiovascular risk assessment with your clinician, not a dementia-specific rationale (general medical guidance).
Address gum health and reduce air pollution exposure where you reasonably can — both are sensible for other established reasons (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • Hope: Modifiable factors offer something people can act on, and genetic subgrouping raises the possibility of targeting prevention to those most likely to benefit.
  • Reality: Most of these findings are observational. Associations in cohort data frequently do not hold up in randomized trials (general medical guidance).

The Reality

  • Reality: The aspirin result is the clearest example of why benefit and harm must be weighed together. In the subgroup where dementia risk fell, serious bleeding rose by a comparable absolute amount (independent fact-check).
  • Reality: The platelet genetic score used in the aspirin analysis is a research tool, not a test you can request from your clinician (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Hearing aids: Studied for cognitive outcomes in mild cognitive impairment, with mixed results across trials and no demonstrated dementia prevention.

Vitamin D: Examined observationally in cohorts at dementia risk; randomized evidence is lacking.

Aspirin: The ASPREE trial was a large placebo-controlled trial in healthy older adults; this analysis examined genetic subgroups within it.

Statins: Examined in people with type 2 diabetes for dementia outcomes.

Periodontitis, air pollution, and glucosamine: Earlier-stage observational and preclinical research (general medical guidance).

Evidence Uncertainty

Aspirin: This was a subgroup analysis defined by genetics, not a prespecified primary result. The researchers themselves call for replication in other populations and a dedicated trial before any clinical application (independent fact-check).

Vitamin D: Observational analysis of self-reported supplement use. Cannot establish that vitamin D caused the cognitive difference (audit correction).

Hearing aids: Results differ across studies. Improvement in cognitive scores among people with mild impairment does not demonstrate prevention of dementia.

Statins: Observational findings in a specific population; confounding by indication is a known limitation in this kind of analysis (general medical guidance).

Periodontitis, air pollution, glucosamine: Early-stage evidence not sufficient to drive individual decisions.

Source note: specific dosing thresholds, trial phases, named compounds, and regulatory and coverage claims in the original draft were not supported by the provided sources and have been removed (audit correction).

What To Watch

Replication of the aspirin genetic finding in other populations, which the researchers identify as the necessary next step.

Randomized evidence on vitamin D and cognition, which currently does not exist at this dose.

Longer-term hearing aid trials with dementia incidence as an endpoint rather than cognitive scores.

Whether earlier-stage findings on periodontitis and air pollution advance to interventional study (general medical guidance).
