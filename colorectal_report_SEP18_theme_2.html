Circulating Tumor DNA (ctDNA) — A Powerful Blood Test to Detect Hidden Cancer After Surgery, But Uncertain Whether It Should Change Your Treatment

"A simple blood test (ctDNA) after colon cancer surgery can detect microscopic residual disease months before scans show recurrence. Patients who test negative have excellent outcomes and may avoid chemotherapy; those who test positive are at high risk. However, major trials show that *acting* on a positive result by giving more or different chemo has not yet improved survival — so guidelines do not yet recommend routine use for treatment decisions outside clinical trials."

The phase 2 PEGASUS trial (11 centers in Italy/Spain) used a blood test (Guardant Reveal) at 2–4 weeks post-surgery to guide adjuvant therapy in high-risk stage II (pT4N0) and stage III (MSS) colon cancer. ctDNA-negative patients received 6 months of capecitabine alone (de-escalation); ctDNA-positive patients received standard 3 months of CAPOX (capecitabine + oxaliplatin). Persistent positivity triggered escalation to FOLFIRI.
The ACS Advisory Council review summarized: in the GALAXY study (>1,000 patients), postoperative ctDNA positivity conferred ~10-fold higher recurrence risk. In Alliance N0147 (stage III), 5-year disease-free survival was 28% if ctDNA-positive vs 77% if negative.
The randomized DYNAMIC trial (stage II) showed ctDNA-guided management cut chemo use from 28% to 15% without compromising 5-year recurrence-free survival (88% vs 87%).
DYNAMIC-III (stage III) tested de-escalation in ctDNA-negative and escalation in ctDNA-positive patients. De-escalation reduced oxaliplatin exposure but missed the non-inferiority threshold (85% vs 88% 3-year RFS). Escalation to FOLFOXIRI did *not* improve ctDNA clearance or recurrence-free survival.
The FIND trial showed ctDNA surveillance detected recurrence ~4 months earlier than imaging, doubling the proportion of recurrences treated with curative intent (48% vs 24%) — but survival benefit is unknown.
ctDNA is a *prognostic* biomarker: it tells you *whether* microscopic cancer remains after surgery. A negative result is highly reassuring (low recurrence risk). A positive result identifies high-risk patients who need close monitoring.
It is *not yet proven* as a *predictive* biomarker: we don't know if changing treatment based on ctDNA (giving more, less, or different chemo) actually improves cure rates. DYNAMIC-III showed escalation didn't help; de-escalation missed its safety margin.
Current NCCN guidelines recognize ctDNA's prognostic value but explicitly *do not recommend* routine use to guide adjuvant treatment decisions or standard surveillance outside clinical trials.
For patients, this means: the test gives valuable risk information, but the "what to do next" remains uncertain and should be discussed in context of a clinical trial or multidisciplinary tumor board.
Patients with resected stage II (especially high-risk pT4N0) or stage III (MSS) colon cancer who have completed surgery with negative margins (R0) and are deciding on adjuvant chemotherapy.
Patients who want more precise recurrence risk stratification than standard pathology (stage, grade, lymph nodes, lymphovascular invasion) provides.
Stage III patients considering whether to accept oxaliplatin (which causes neuropathy) — a negative ctDNA may support a discussion about shorter or omitted oxaliplatin, though this is not yet standard.
Patients in surveillance after curative treatment who want earlier recurrence detection than CT scans provide.
Ask your oncologist: "Is ctDNA (minimal residual disease) testing available at this center, and would the result change my treatment plan?" — note that NCCN does not yet endorse it for routine treatment decisions.
If you have stage II colon cancer with no other high-risk features, ask: "Could a negative ctDNA result allow me to safely skip adjuvant chemotherapy?" — reference the DYNAMIC trial.
If you have stage III disease, ask: "Should I get ctDNA testing after surgery to help decide on chemo duration or intensity?" — understand that escalation based on positivity has not been proven beneficial.
If you choose ctDNA testing, insist on serial testing (multiple time points) — single time-point results are less reliable; PEGASUS required two consecutive negatives (LB1 and LB2) to de-escalate.
Request that ctDNA results be reviewed in a multidisciplinary tumor board or within a clinical trial context (e.g., NCT04259944 PEGASUS-type protocols).

The Hope

  • Hope: A blood test can spare some patients unnecessary chemotherapy (stage II) and detect recurrence earlier when it's potentially curable.
  • Reality: For stage III — the group where chemo clearly saves lives — we don't yet know how to safely use ctDNA results to reduce treatment without risking cure. Escalation based on positive ctDNA didn't improve outcomes in DYNAMIC-III.

The Reality

  • Cost: ctDNA tests are expensive; serial testing multiplies cost. Insurance coverage for using ctDNA to guide adjuvant treatment decisions is inconsistent (general medical guidance).
  • Access: Not all centers offer ctDNA testing. Tumor-informed assays require tissue banking and have longer turnaround times. Tumor-naive assays are faster but may differ in sensitivity (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Guardant Reveal (Guardant Health): Plasma-only, methylation-based ctDNA assay [a blood test that detects tiny fragments of tumor DNA] used in PEGASUS; no tumor tissue needed; reports 'detected' or 'not detected'; investigational.

Signatera (Natera): Tumor-informed assay (requires tissue); widely used in GI oncology (general medical guidance).

Standard adjuvant regimens: CAPOX (3 months) or FOLFOX (6 months) for stage III; capecitabine alone or observation for stage II. ctDNA-guided de-escalation/escalation remains investigational (general medical guidance).

Ongoing trials: Multiple phase III studies (including DYNAMIC-III follow-up) are testing ctDNA-guided strategies in stage II/III and metastatic settings.

Evidence Uncertainty

Strongest evidence: ctDNA positivity after curative surgery = ~10x recurrence risk (GALAXY); 5-year DFS 28% vs 77% in stage III (Alliance N0147); lead time ~4–7 months before radiographic recurrence (FIND, Signatera studies).

Key uncertainty: Treatment modification based on ctDNA has not improved outcomes in stage III (DYNAMIC-III: escalation failed, de-escalation missed non-inferiority). Stage II de-escalation worked (DYNAMIC) but longer follow-up needed.

Technical limitations: Assays differ (tumor-informed vs tumor-naïve, methylation vs mutation-based). Sensitivity for low-volume disease varies. False negatives occur — "ctDNA negative" does not guarantee cure.

PEGASUS limitations: Single-arm phase 2; historical control (TOSCA trial); immature OS and quality-of-life data; ctDNA assay (Guardant Reveal vL1.2) is investigational.

What To Watch

DYNAMIC-III final analysis and mature overall survival data — will clarify if de-escalation is truly safe in stage III.

PEGASUS phase 3 expansion or similar randomized trials comparing ctDNA-guided vs standard adjuvant therapy.

NCCN guideline updates — watch for shift from "prognostic only" to "consider for treatment guidance" with specific clinical scenarios.

Medicare coverage decisions for serial ctDNA monitoring in adjuvant and surveillance settings.
