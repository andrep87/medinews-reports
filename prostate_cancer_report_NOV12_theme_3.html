Five-Day Radiation (SBRT) Becomes Standard Option for Localized Prostate Cancer

"New ASTRO guidelines endorse ultrahypofractionated radiation (SBRT) — just 5 treatments over 1–2 weeks — as a standard option for low- and intermediate-risk localized prostate cancer, offering equivalent cancer control to surgery or longer radiation with better preservation of urinary and sexual function."

The American Society for Radiation Oncology (ASTRO) updated its 2018 guideline, now recommending ultrahypofractionated external beam radiation therapy (UHF-EBRT, also called SBRT) as a standard treatment for men with low- or intermediate-risk localized prostate cancer, completing treatment in 5 sessions over 1–2 weeks. (general medical guidance)
The pivotal PACE-B trial (published 2024 in NEJM) showed 5-session SBRT was noninferior to moderate hypofractionation (20–28 sessions) or conventional therapy (39–45 sessions) for 5-year freedom from biochemical or clinical failure. (general medical guidance)
The PACE-A trial randomised men to surgery versus five-session SBRT and reported equivalent cancer control, with substantially less urinary leakage in the SBRT group. (general medical guidance)
Moderate hypofractionation (20–28 sessions over 4–6 weeks) remains recommended across all risk groups; conventional fractionation (39–45 sessions) is no longer preferred. (general medical guidance)
Patients can now choose a curative treatment that takes 1–2 weeks instead of 8–9 weeks, dramatically reducing time away from work, travel burden, and treatment fatigue — without compromising cancer outcomes.
SBRT uses advanced imaging (MRI-guided planning, daily cone-beam CT, fiducial markers, rectal spacers) to deliver high-dose radiation with millimeter precision, sparing the bladder, rectum, and erectile nerves better than older techniques. (general medical guidance)
Patient-reported outcome research shows clinicians often underestimate urinary and bowel symptoms; whether SBRT produces better functional outcomes than longer courses is not settled by those findings alone (general medical guidance).
Men with unfavorable intermediate-risk or high-risk disease may still be offered SBRT, but the guideline gives a "conditional recommendation" due to less data; discussion with a radiation oncologist is essential. (general medical guidance)
Age and comorbidity alone do not exclude SBRT; shared decision-making incorporating patient preferences, anatomy, and baseline function is emphasized. (general medical guidance)
You may wish to ask your urologist and radiation oncologist: "Am I a candidate for 5-treatment SBRT? How do the urinary and sexual function outcomes compare to surgery for my specific anatomy?" (general medical guidance)
You may wish to request a rectal spacer (e.g., SpaceOAR hydrogel) consultation if choosing radiation — it reduces rectal dose and long-term bowel toxicity, and is standard with modern SBRT. (general medical guidance)
You may wish to ask whether your team records your own reported symptoms (urinary, bowel and sexual function) before treatment and at follow-up visits, since research shows clinicians and patients often disagree about severity (general medical guidance).
Verify your radiation center uses MRI-guided planning, daily image guidance, and fiducial markers; these are quality benchmarks for safe SBRT delivery. (general medical guidance)

The Hope

  • Hope: Many patients complete curative treatment in a single week, return to normal life quickly, and maintain excellent urinary continence and sexual function — outcomes comparable to or better than surgery in modern series.
  • Reality: SBRT requires specialized equipment and expertise; not all centers offer true MRI-guided, fiducial-tracked SBRT. Acute urinary irritation (frequency, urgency) can be more intense during weeks 1–4 but typically resolves. (general medical guidance)

The Reality

  • "Equivalent cancer control" means similar biochemical failure rates; long-term metastasis-free and overall survival data beyond 10 years are still accumulating for SBRT specifically. (general medical guidance)
  • Cost: SBRT may have higher upfront facility charges but lower total societal cost (fewer visits, less time off work); insurance coverage varies — verify pre-authorization. (general medical guidance)

Evidence Uncertainty

The PACE-B trial found that five-session SBRT was non-inferior to longer courses for cancer control, with comparable late side effects. (general medical guidance)

The PACE-A trial randomised men to surgery versus five-session SBRT and reported equivalent cancer control, with substantially less urinary leakage in the SBRT group. (general medical guidance)

Uncertainties: Long-term (>10 year) SBRT data still maturing; optimal dose/fractionation for unfavorable intermediate/high-risk; impact of SBRT on salvage therapy options if recurrence occurs; generalizability to centers without MRI-guided SBRT expertise. (general medical guidance)

HYPO-RT-PC revealed persistent clinician underestimation of urinary/bowel symptoms up to 4 years — underscores need for mandatory PROMs in routine practice.
