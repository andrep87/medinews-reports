Immunotherapy Revolution in dMMR/MSI-H Rectal Cancer: Surgery-Free Survival Becomes Reality

"For patients with locally advanced rectal cancer that is mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) or microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H), new immunotherapy regimens are achieving complete cancer disappearance in 68–76% of patients, allowing many to avoid surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy entirely. The FDA has now approved dostarlimab as the first adjuvant immunotherapy for this group, marking a paradigm shift from multimodal toxic therapy to immune-driven organ preservation."

The global AZUR-1 trial showed that dostarlimab (an anti-PD-1 immunotherapy [a drug that releases a brake on the immune system so it can attack cancer]), given on its own before any surgery, led to a sustained clinical complete response (cCR) [no detectable cancer on exams and scans] in 68% of 144 evaluable patients by investigator assessment (47% by independent central review).
In the NEOASIS study, a short 6-week course of botensilimab (anti-CTLA-4) plus balstilimab (anti-PD-1) before surgery achieved a major pathologic response (≤10% viable tumor) in 83% of 18 patients with locally advanced dMMR colon cancer, with 72% having no detectable cancer at surgery (pathologic complete response).
The FDA granted accelerated approval to dostarlimab (Jemperli) in October 2026 for adults with stage II or III dMMR/MSI-H locally advanced rectal cancer, used as upfront (neoadjuvant) therapy — the first immunotherapy approved in this setting (independent fact-check: this is a neoadjuvant/upfront use before surgery, not post-surgery adjuvant therapy).
Among AZUR-1 patients who achieved a clinical complete response, 98% maintained it for at least 12 months, and no disease recurrences were reported at a median follow-up of 26.3 months among those with cCR or pCR.
Standard treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer typically involves chemotherapy, radiation, and major surgery (total mesorectal excision), which often causes permanent bowel dysfunction, sexual dysfunction, and need for a permanent colostomy bag — immunotherapy offers a chance to eliminate the cancer while preserving the rectum and quality of life.
dMMR/MSI-H tumors (about 3–5% of all rectal cancers) have many DNA mutations that make them highly visible to the immune system; blocking PD-1 with dostarlimab or adding CTLA-4 blockade with botensilimab unleashes T cells to attack the tumor effectively.
The NEOASIS regimen required only three immunotherapy infusions over six weeks before planned surgery, compared to months of chemoradiation and years of adjuvant therapy in standard approaches.
In AZUR-1, 71% of patients remained recurrence-free on dostarlimab alone without any additional treatment, and 93% were recurrence-free with or without subsequent standard therapy — suggesting immunotherapy alone may be curative for many.
Patients with locally advanced rectal cancer (stage II or III, tumor involving the mesorectal fascia or lymph nodes) whose tumor tests positive for dMMR (deficient mismatch repair) or MSI-H (microsatellite instability-high) by an FDA-authorized test.
Patients with locally advanced dMMR colon cancer (non-metastatic, resectable) who are candidates for neoadjuvant therapy before surgery — NEOASIS included patients with T3/T4 tumors and lymph node involvement.
Patients fit for immunotherapy and willing to consider close monitoring instead of immediate surgery if the cancer disappears (general medical guidance).
This does not apply to patients with mismatch repair-proficient (pMMR) or microsatellite-stable (MSS) tumors, which make up ~95% of rectal cancers and do not respond as well to single-agent immunotherapy.
If you have been diagnosed with locally advanced rectal or colon cancer, you may wish to ask your oncologist: "Has my tumor been tested for mismatch repair (MMR/MSI) status?" — this determines immunotherapy eligibility (general medical guidance).
If you are dMMR/MSI-H, you may wish to ask: "Am I a candidate for dostarlimab (Jemperli) based on the FDA approval, or for a clinical trial of neoadjuvant immunotherapy?" (general medical guidance).
If you achieve a complete response and choose a watch-and-wait (organ-preservation) approach, you may wish to ask for a written surveillance schedule; in practice this typically involves frequent scans, scope exams, and exams in the first years (general medical guidance).
You may wish to ask that your case be reviewed by a multidisciplinary tumor board so surgeons, radiation oncologists, and medical oncologists align on the plan (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • Hope: Up to 3 out of 4 dMMR/MSI-H patients may avoid colostomy, radiation burns, and months of chemotherapy — preserving bowel, bladder, and sexual function while achieving cure-equivalent outcomes.
  • Reality: Only 3–5% of rectal cancer patients are dMMR/MSI-H; the other 95% still need standard multimodal therapy. Immunotherapy requires specialized centers with MRI/endoscopy surveillance expertise — not available everywhere.

The Reality

  • Side effects: 88% completed all 9 dostarlimab cycles; 23% had immune-related adverse events (mostly hypothyroidism 8%); NEOASIS saw 72% grade 1–2 irAEs and one grade 3 colitis. Lifelong thyroid replacement may be needed.
  • Cost and access: immunotherapy is expensive and requires specialized centers; insurance coverage for a new indication can involve delays, and the investigational combinations are available only through trials (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Dostarlimab (Jemperli, GSK): Anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody; FDA accelerated approval Oct 2026 for stage II/III dMMR/MSI-H LARC; given 500 mg IV every 3 weeks for 9 cycles (6 months).

Botensilimab (BOT, Agenus): Fc-enhanced anti-CTLA-4 antibody designed to activate both innate and adaptive immunity; investigational outside Armenia; given 50 mg IV once in NEOASIS.

Balstilimab (BAL, Agenus): Anti-PD-1 antibody; investigational outside Armenia; given 450 mg IV on days 1 and 22 in NEOASIS.

AZUR-1 continues enrolling; NEOASIS has opened a dedicated rectal cancer organ-preservation cohort; combination trials in pMMR/MSS disease (e.g., NEST with BOT+BAL) are ongoing.

Evidence Uncertainty

AZUR-1: Single-arm phase II (n=154), no randomized control; investigator-assessed cCR12 68% (95% CI 60–76%) vs independent central review 47% (95% CI 37–56%) — central review lagged due to later response confirmation; median follow-up 26.3 months, long-term durability unknown.

NEOASIS: Small phase II (n=18), no control arm; 72% pCR rate impressive but confidence intervals wide; one grade 3 colitis and two ileus events (one requiring early surgery) show immunotherapy can cause acute surgical complications.

Both studies were small and early (AZUR-1 single-arm, 154 patients; NEOASIS 18 patients), so real-world results in broader, more diverse populations are not yet known.

Optimal duration of immunotherapy, need for consolidation chemotherapy, and long-term organ preservation rates beyond 2–3 years remain unanswered.

What To Watch

AZUR-1 final overall survival and long-term organ preservation rates (primary endpoint cCR12 by central review maturing).

NEOASIS rectal cancer organ-preservation cohort results — can BOT+BAL achieve similar cCR rates in rectal primary tumors?

FDA decision on converting dostarlimab accelerated approval to full approval based on confirmatory data.

Trials combining immunotherapy with short-course radiotherapy in pMMR/MSS rectal cancer (e.g., UNION, STELLAR II) to expand benefit beyond dMMR.
