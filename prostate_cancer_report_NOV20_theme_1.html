American Cancer Society Issues First Direct Recommendation for Routine PSA Screening in 16 Years

"The American Cancer Society now directly recommends regular PSA [prostate-specific antigen, a protein made by the prostate] blood tests for men ages 50–69 every two years, and yearly for higher-risk men starting at age 45, replacing its earlier "shared decision-making" approach[cite: 3, 4]. This update reflects stronger evidence that screening lowers metastatic [spread to distant parts of the body] disease and prostate cancer deaths, while newer tools like MRI [magnetic resonance imaging, a detailed scan using magnets and radio waves] and biomarkers [biological signs or tests in blood, urine, or tissue] help reduce unneeded biopsies [tissue samples][cite: 3, 4]."

The American Cancer Society (ACS) released its first prostate cancer screening guideline update since 2010, moving from "discuss with your doctor" to a direct recommendation for routine PSA [prostate-specific antigen, a protein made by the prostate] blood testing[cite: 3, 4].
Men at average risk ages 50–69 should get a PSA test every two years, while higher-risk men—Black men, men with a father, brother, or son diagnosed before age 65, or men with multiple affected relatives at any age—should start yearly screening at age 45[cite: 3, 4].
Men with one relative diagnosed at age 65 or older screen every two years from ages 45–69, men ages 70–74 decide individually with their doctor, and screening is not recommended at age 75 or older or when life expectancy is under 10 years (independent fact-check: the ACS 2026 guideline generally advises against routine screening at 75+, with individualized discussion in select very healthy men ages 75–79 and discouraging it after age 80 or with less than 10 years life expectancy)[cite: 3, 4].
The guidelines advise repeating a high PSA result before taking further steps, using MRI and/or validated biomarker tests to help decide who needs a biopsy, and choosing active surveillance [close monitoring rather than immediate treatment] for low-risk cancer[cite: 3, 4].
Long-term follow-up from the European ERSPC [European Randomized Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer] trial showed screening reduces prostate cancer deaths by 13% and lowers metastatic disease, while the U.S. PLCO trial had heavy "control" group screening that diluted differences[cite: 3, 4].
Stepwise checks—repeating an elevated PSA, adding MRI or biomarkers—can spare many men unnecessary biopsies and avoid treating slow-growing tumors that would never cause harm[cite: 3, 4].
Black men experience 67% higher incidence [rate of new cases] and nearly double the death rate (with one source noting up to threefold), so earlier, annual testing starting at 45 aims to catch aggressive cancer sooner[cite: 3, 4].
Clearer guidance gives primary care clinicians a straightforward starting point instead of open-ended debate, helping more men who might otherwise be missed get timely evaluation[cite: 3, 4].
Men ages 50–69 at average risk (not Black and without strong family history) are advised to have a PSA blood test every two years[cite: 3, 4].
Black men and men with a close relative (father, brother, or son) diagnosed before age 65, or multiple affected relatives at any age, are advised to start annual PSA testing at age 45[cite: 3, 4].
Men with a single close relative diagnosed at age 65 or older are advised to screen every two years from ages 45–69, while men ages 70–74 make an individualized choice based on overall health and life expectancy[cite: 3, 4].
Screening is not recommended for men aged 75 and older or those whose health limits life expectancy to less than 10 years[cite: 3, 4].
If you are aged 50–69 and have not had a PSA test in two years, you may wish to schedule one at your next primary care visit; if you are aged 45–49 and Black or have a family history of prostate cancer, you may wish to ask about starting PSA testing now[cite: 3, 4].
If your PSA comes back elevated (such as at least 3 ng/mL [nanograms per milliliter] for ages 45–69 or at least 6.5 ng/mL for ages 70–74), you may wish to ask about repeating the test in two weeks to three months before deciding on a biopsy[cite: 3, 4].
If a biopsy is being considered after an elevated PSA, you may wish to ask your doctor whether a prostate MRI or a validated biomarker test fits your situation[cite: 3, 4] (general medical guidance).
If you are diagnosed with low-risk prostate cancer, you may wish to ask about active surveillance and what follow-up schedule of PSA checks, MRI scans, or repeat biopsies is recommended[cite: 3, 4] (general medical guidance).

The Hope

  • Hope: Catching aggressive prostate cancer earlier saves lives, and using MRI and biomarkers can reduce unneeded biopsies and side effects such as urinary leakage or erection problems[cite: 3, 4] (general medical guidance).
  • Reality: Screening can still detect slow-growing tumors that never needed treatment, and active surveillance involves repeated tests and appointments that can feel stressful[cite: 3, 4] (general medical guidance).

The Reality

  • Hope: Earlier annual screening starting at age 45 offers a focused way to address higher risks in Black men and men with family history[cite: 3, 4].
  • Reality: Real-world coverage and access to timely follow-up care can vary depending on a person's health plan and local care setting[cite: 3, 4] (general medical guidance).

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

PSA blood testing remains the main screening test, while a digital rectal exam [a physical exam of the prostate through the rectum] is no longer recommended on its own as a screening test[cite: 3, 4].

When PSA is elevated, the updated care path centers on repeating the PSA test, then using MRI and/or validated biomarker tests before deciding on a targeted biopsy[cite: 3, 4].

Active surveillance using regular monitoring instead of immediate surgery or radiation is an established approach for low-risk disease to protect quality of life[cite: 3, 4] (general medical guidance).

Evidence Uncertainty

The strongest mortality evidence comes from the ERSPC trial with more than 20 years of follow-up (showing a 13% overall reduction in prostate cancer death, with larger reductions reported in select European centers with organized screening), while exact figures for lifelong screening starting at ages 45 or 50 rely on modeling estimates[cite: 3, 4].

Using higher PSA thresholds and MRI triage helps balance benefits and harms, though overdiagnosis [finding slow-growing cancers that would never cause symptoms] still occurs, especially in older men or those with other serious illnesses[cite: 3, 4].

The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) [an independent federal health panel] has previously used a more cautious individualized stance for men ages 55–69, so clinicians and patients may still see differing guidance across organizations[cite: 3, 4] (general medical guidance).

What To Watch

Future guideline updates from other major groups, such as the USPSTF, and how primary care clinics put the new ACS schedule into practice[cite: 3, 4] (general medical guidance).

Ongoing research into screening pathways, including imaging and blood or urine markers, aimed at improving accuracy and limiting overdiagnosis[cite: 3, 4] (general medical guidance).
