First 90 Days on Antipsychotics Are a Critical Window for Diabetes Risk

"A massive UK study of nearly 97,000 adults with serious mental illness found that the first three months after starting common antipsychotic medications (olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone, aripiprazole) carry the highest risk for rapid blood sugar rise and weight gain. HbA1c increased 14 times faster and weight gain occurred 9 times faster during this window compared to before treatment. People with prediabetes were especially vulnerable — 19% developed type 2 diabetes within two years versus 3% with normal glucose."

A UK study (Lancet Psychiatry) of 96,849 adults aged 18-59 with serious mental illness found that the first 90 days after starting a second-generation antipsychotic (olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone, or aripiprazole) carry the fastest rise in blood sugar and weight (independent fact-check: figures verified against the primary publication).
They tracked weight and HbA1c for two years before and two years after starting the medication. The rate of HbA1c increase was ~14 times higher, and weight gain rate ~9 times higher, in the first 90 days versus the pre-treatment period.
Olanzapine was associated with the largest early increases in both HbA1c and weight. Women had faster early rises than men.
Among those with prediabetes at baseline, 19% progressed to type 2 diabetes within two years, compared to 3% of those with normal glucose.
Antipsychotics are essential for treating schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and other serious conditions — but their metabolic side effects contribute to the 15–20 year life expectancy gap in this population.
The first 90 days represent a "metabolic emergency window" where proactive monitoring and intervention could prevent or delay diabetes onset in thousands of patients annually.
Weight gain alone is not a reliable signal: some patients developed rising blood sugar without proportional weight gain, meaning clinicians must check HbA1c directly, not just weigh patients.
The mechanism involves increased appetite, altered energy balance, and direct insulin resistance in muscle and liver — laboratory research confirms olanzapine impairs insulin signaling in muscle cells independently of mitochondrial effects.
Anyone starting a second-generation antipsychotic (especially olanzapine, but also quetiapine, risperidone, aripiprazole) for schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or other indications.
Patients with pre-existing prediabetes (HbA1c 5.7–6.4%), overweight/obesity, family history of diabetes, or gestational diabetes history — they face the highest absolute risk.
Women may warrant closer monitoring given faster early metabolic changes observed in the study.
Clinicians prescribing these medications in any setting (psychiatry, primary care, hospital discharge).
If you or someone you care for is starting an antipsychotic, you may wish to ask the prescriber about checking weight and blood sugar (HbA1c) before starting and during the first few months, since this is when changes happen fastest (general medical guidance).
You may wish to ask whether a lower-metabolic-risk antipsychotic is appropriate for your situation, recognizing that drug choice depends on which controls symptoms best (general medical guidance).
If you have prediabetes, you may wish to discuss the higher risk and closer monitoring with your care team, since this group was especially affected (general medical guidance).
You may wish to ask about lifestyle support (diet, activity) early in treatment rather than waiting (general medical guidance).
Evidence Badges
[1] Large Observational Cohort Study (NHS Primary Care Records, n≈97,000) [Published in The Lancet Psychiatry] [University College London / NHS England Data] [2] Independent Sources (Diabetes.co.uk, Medical Xpress)

The Hope

  • Mental health stability comes first — never stop or reduce antipsychotics without psychiatric guidance. The goal is safer prescribing and monitoring, not avoidance of necessary treatment.
  • Metabolic monitoring in mental health settings remains inconsistent; many patients fall through cracks between psychiatry and primary care. Integrated care models are needed but not yet standard.

The Reality

  • Olanzapine is often the most effective for treatment-resistant symptoms; risk-benefit decisions are individualized. The data inform monitoring intensity, not absolute contraindication.
  • Health disparities: People with serious mental illness face barriers to nutrition, exercise, and preventive care that amplify drug effects. System-level solutions are essential.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

Lower-metabolic-risk antipsychotics: Aripiprazole, ziprasidone, lurasidone, cariprazine generally have less weight/glucose impact than olanzapine or quetiapine — but efficacy for specific symptoms varies; switching must be psychiatrist-guided. (general medical guidance)

Metformin co-prescription: Some guidelines recommend prophylactic metformin at antipsychotic initiation for high-risk patients; evidence is mixed but growing.

GLP-1 receptor agonists: Increasingly used off-label for antipsychotic-induced weight gain; formal trials in this population are underway.

Non-pharmacologic: Structured lifestyle programs (nutrition, exercise, behavioral support) adapted for serious mental illness show promise but are rarely integrated into mental health care.

Evidence Uncertainty

Strengths: Enormous real-world sample (97,000), long follow-up (2 years pre/post), representative NHS population, multiple antipsychotics compared, time-varying analysis.

Limitations: Observational — cannot prove causation; confounding by indication (sicker patients may get olanzapine); no data on diet, activity, smoking, or other medications; HbA1c/weight measured intermittently per clinical practice, not protocol.

The "14x faster" and "9x faster" refer to rates of change (slope), not absolute magnitudes — actual HbA1c increases were modest in absolute terms but clinically meaningful at population scale.

What To Watch

Updated NICE/ADA/psychiatric guidelines incorporating 90-day intensive monitoring recommendations for antipsychotic initiators.

Trials of early metformin or GLP-1 co-therapy at antipsychotic initiation in high-risk patients (several recruiting now).

Research on genetic/biomarker predictors of who develops metabolic side effects — could enable personalized antipsychotic selection.

Long-term outcomes: Does early intensive monitoring actually reduce diabetes incidence, cardiovascular events, and mortality in this population?
