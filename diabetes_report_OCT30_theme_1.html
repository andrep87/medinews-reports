Intensive Diet Plus Supervised Exercise Can Put Early-Onset Type 2 Diabetes Into Remission

"A landmark international trial found that 54% of young adults (ages 18–45) with type 2 diabetes achieved remission — normal blood sugar off all diabetes medications for at least 3 months — after a 6-month program combining a very low-calorie meal-replacement diet with supervised aerobic and resistance exercise. Critically, participants lost fat while preserving muscle mass, a key advantage over diet alone."

The RESET for Remission trial enrolled 96 young adults (ages 18-45) with early-onset type 2 diabetes and obesity, who were not on insulin, at three centers in England and Canada. Half received an intensive program; half received usual care.
For the first 12 weeks, the intervention group consumed 800–900 calories daily via meal-replacement shakes and bars, plus supervised aerobic and resistance exercise twice weekly with a trainer and once weekly independently.
For the next 12 weeks, they transitioned to a healthy whole-food diet designed to maintain weight loss and continued exercising independently.
After 24 weeks, 54% of the intensive-program group (27 of 50) achieved diabetes remission — normal blood sugar (HbA1c below 6.5%) off all diabetes medications for at least 12 weeks — versus just 4% (2 of 46) with usual care, making them about 21 times more likely to reach remission (independent fact-check: figures verified against the primary Lancet publication).
Early-onset type 2 diabetes (diagnosed before 45) progresses faster, leads to complications sooner, and shortens life expectancy more than later-onset disease — yet remission options have been limited for this age group.
Unlike diet-only approaches, adding supervised exercise preserved muscle mass and strength while participants lost an average of 7.5 kg more than controls. MRI scans confirmed >9% liver fat loss and >24% visceral (organ) fat loss — the fat deposits that drive insulin resistance.
Participants also improved fitness by ~10%, lowered blood pressure and resting heart rate, and reported less depression, less diabetes distress, and better quality of life.
This is the first randomized trial to prove that combining structured exercise with a low-energy diet achieves high remission rates while protecting muscle — a critical factor for long-term metabolic health and frailty prevention.
Adults aged 18–45 with type 2 diabetes and obesity (BMI ≥30, or ≥27.5 in some Asian populations) who are medically stable enough for a very low-calorie diet and moderate exercise.
People motivated to pursue remission and able to commit to ~3 hours of supervised exercise weekly plus dietary structure for at least 12 weeks.
Patients not on insulin or able to safely pause insulin under medical supervision; the trial excluded insulin users.
Those planning pregnancy may especially benefit, as weight-loss medications are not recommended during pregnancy but this lifestyle approach is.
If you are a younger adult with recently diagnosed type 2 diabetes and obesity, you may wish to ask your doctor whether a structured, medically supervised low-calorie-diet-plus-exercise program (like RESET) could help you aim for remission (general medical guidance).
Because very-low-calorie diets require adjusting some diabetes medications to avoid low blood sugar, you may wish to ask your care team to supervise any medication changes — not to adjust doses on your own (general medical guidance).
You may wish to ask about combining aerobic and resistance (strength) exercise, which the trial used to help preserve muscle during weight loss (general medical guidance).
You may wish to ask for referral to a dietitian and, if available, a supervised exercise program or physical therapist (general medical guidance).
Evidence Badges
Randomized, open-label, blinded-endpoint trial (RESET for Remission, 96 patients) Published in The Lancet Regional Health - Americas; presented at EASD 2026 Three centers (England and Canada); McGill / Leicester / Alberta 3 Independent Sources

The Hope

  • Remission is not a cure — diabetes can return if weight is regained or lifestyle slips. Ongoing maintenance requires sustained healthy eating and regular activity.
  • Very-low-calorie diets are hard: hunger, social disruption, and fatigue are real. Supervised programs with meal replacements improve adherence but are not widely available or always covered by insurance.

The Reality

  • Muscle preservation is a major win, but the trial didn't measure bone density — rapid weight loss can affect bone health, especially in younger adults still building peak bone mass.
  • Access is the biggest barrier: few health systems offer integrated diet+exercise remission programs. Advocacy and policy change are needed to make this standard of care.

Deep Dive Analysis

Research Progress

The intervention itself is a structured lifestyle protocol, not a drug: meal replacements (e.g., Optifast, Counterweight, or equivalent), supervised exercise protocol, and behavioral support.

GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, tirzepatide) achieve 15–20% weight loss but effects on remission rates in young adults and muscle preservation are less established; long-term safety over decades unknown.

Bariatric surgery achieves high remission rates but is invasive, has waitlists, and is not recommended during pregnancy planning.

Next step: A larger, longer trial comparing diet alone vs. diet + exercise in real-world settings to confirm exercise is the active ingredient for muscle preservation and to test scalability.

Evidence Uncertainty

Strengths: Randomized, blinded endpoint assessment, multi-ethnic cohort (Montreal, Edmonton, Leicester), objective measures (MRI for fat/muscle, fitness testing), high remission rate (54%) at upper end of similar trials.

The trial enrolled people not on insulin with obesity, so results may not apply to everyone with early-onset type 2 diabetes; longer-term durability of remission beyond the study period is being followed (general medical guidance).

Early side effects (constipation, headache, dizziness, fatigue) were common but transient; no serious adverse events reported.

What To Watch

Larger pragmatic trials testing diet+exercise remission programs in community settings (primary care, community health centers) with longer follow-up (2–5 years).

Studies examining whether GLP-1 medications combined with supervised exercise can achieve similar remission rates with better tolerability.

Research on remission durability: what predicts who stays in remission vs. relapses? Behavioral, metabolic, and microbiome markers under investigation.

Health economic analyses to support insurance coverage for structured remission programs as a cost-effective alternative to lifelong medication escalation.
